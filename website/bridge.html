Why Shape Matters, and How the Two Halves Connect

Why the project changes the shape of MOFs, how the earlier spectrum-based classification relates to morphology, a new test of whether the spectrum sees particle shape, and how the first half can be reused.

1Why We Want to Change the Shape of a MOF

You know
How a unit cell gives faces and how the faces give a shape (Morphology), and how stretching the cell gives plates and rods (Cell Deform).
This page adds
The reason the project cares about shape at all, and how the earlier spectrum work connects to it.
Which lets us
Keep the earlier work as part of one story, not as a separate project that was abandoned.

In simple words

A MOF is a sponge made of crystal. Gas or liquid molecules have to get into its pores. The outside shape of a crystal particle decides three things that matter for that job: which kinds of surface are open to the outside, how far a molecule must travel to reach the inside, and how the particles pack together in a filter, a film or a tablet. Two crystals of exactly the same MOF can therefore behave differently only because one is a cube and the other a thin plate. If we can predict, from the structure alone, which shapes a MOF can take and what makes it take each one, we can choose the shape that works best for adsorption before spending weeks in the laboratory. This is the link to the original aim of the project: to improve how strongly and how selectively a MOF adsorbs, by working on the computational side of how MOFs are made.

In technical words

  • Exposed faces. Different crystal faces expose different pore openings, metal sites and linker orientations, so adsorption, catalysis and growth happen at different rates on different faces. The surface-energy ranking of faces (Phase 4) decides which are large.
  • Diffusion path. Uptake into a pore channel is diffusion-limited, and the time to fill scales as t ∝ L²/D, where L is the length of the channel the molecule must follow and D the diffusion coefficient. A plate or rod whose short side lies along the channel reduces L and so the uptake time. This is the standard diffusion scaling, not a result of this project.
  • Packing and processing. Aspect ratio and facet shape set how crystals pack, how a membrane or film orients, and how a pellet behaves.
  • The panel's question. A morphology is the output that connects a unit cell to a usable particle; it is why the panel asked for several morphologies from one established cell.
These are reasons taken from standard crystal-engineering and transport arguments. This project has not computed adsorption or diffusion for any shape. What it does compute is which shapes a MOF can take and, with limits, which one is favoured.

3What the Two Halves Share

ObjectSpectrum workMorphology work

Size confoundΔα grows with graph sizeA crystal has a size and a shape; a torus has neither
QuestionWhich MOFs deserve simulation?Which shape will a given MOF take, and is it a useful one?

The size confound is the opening for the test on the next page. Every earlier spectrum was measured on a torus: the graph was tiled with wrap-around, so it has no edge, no surface and no shape. A real crystal particle is finite and has faces. If the spectrum is to say anything about real particles, it has to be measured on a finite graph, and a finite graph can have a shape.

Whether the spectrum and the crystal shape are actually related is tested on its own page, Spectrum ↔ Shape: three calculations, each produced by the notebook code/spectrum_shape_relation/Spectrum_Shape_Relation.ipynb.
