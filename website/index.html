1The Project in Brief

A metal–organic framework (MOF) is a crystal made of metal clusters joined by organic linkers. It is full of small pores, so it can store or separate gases such as CO2. The project has two parts.

Part I asked a screening question. Hundreds of thousands of MOFs have been proposed, and checking one by molecular simulation takes hours. We asked whether a single number computed from a MOF's connectivity alone, in seconds, could pick out the frameworks worth simulating. We built each framework's periodic graph from its crystal file and computed the width Δα of its multifractal spectrum, for 138 frameworks (77 hypothetical and 61 synthesised). The answer is no, as defined: Δα keeps growing as the analysed piece of crystal grows, so it measures the size of the calculation more than the framework. The bands built from it are withdrawn, with their reasons, on the Results page. The graph pipeline itself is verified and is reused in Part II.

Part II answers the panel's question. After Panel Review 1 the panel asked whether a few crystal shapes (morphologies) can be generated from the unit cell of an established MOF, mathematically and computationally. They can, in three steps:

  1. The symmetry of the unit cell decides which crystal faces may exist (16 propositions, proved on The mathematics).
  2. Those faces give a family of possible shapes: for MOF-5, HKUST-1 and UiO-66 the family runs cube → cuboctahedron → octahedron, and for ZIF-8 cube → rhombic dodecahedron (Morphology). Changing the cell lengths adds plates and rods (Changing the cell).
  3. A growth model picks the likely member of the family: the BFDH rule from the plane spacings, Wulff shapes from surface energies, and several variants (Surface energy, Faces from the graph).

How good is it? The predictions were scored against 134 laboratory records from 35 papers on 12 MOFs (Against experiment). For crystals grown without an additive, the structure alone gives the main crystal face correctly for 4 of 5 MOFs (UiO-66 is the exception), as often as the most common laboratory answer for each MOF, but without using any laboratory data. For crystals grown with an additive no model is reliable, because the additive changes the shape in a way no structure-only model contains. Every shape can be placed on one exact map of cubic crystal shapes, and all six laboratory series that change shape on that map follow the predicted order (The morphology map).

What it is for. The Shape advisor brings this together for ten MOFs: the predicted shape in 3D, what laboratories saw, how much each face's energy would have to fall to change the shape, and how open each face is to CO2 and N2. This links crystal shape back to the original aim, adsorption, as geometry; adsorption itself is not computed.

What is still open. The surface energies have not yet been checked against DFT, the reference quantum-mechanical method (six slabs are prepared; the calculation needs HPC time). The relaxed rerun of the team's CHGNet pipeline needs a GPU. A second, independent reading of a sample of the laboratory records is planned.

The band does not converge, at any tested range of q, and should not be read as a structural result — for two separate reasons. Δα is measured over an exponent q ∈ [−10, 10]; at the extremes of that range the measurement is dominated by a single box, so Δα inherits a term that grows with supercell size and never settles. We tested whether narrowing the q range removes this dependence, on four frameworks spanning four different nets (pcu, tbo, sod, fcu), and it does not: at every cap tried (|q| ≤ 10, 8, 5, 4, 3, 2) Δα continued to drift as the supercell grew, by more than the 5% tolerance we set for calling it settled. Separately, both bands below were in fact computed on the atomic supercell graph rather than the coarse-grained graph this site describes — a graph that spans a molecular and a framework length scale, across which a single power-law fit is not valid regardless of size. The numbers are kept, and the method that produced them documented in full, on the Results page and in the dataset README, exactly as the two withdrawn claims below were kept rather than deleted.
Both datasets were re-fetched in full (all 77 hMOF, all 61 CoRE MOF) and re-analysed on the coarse-grained supercell graph — the correct one. 72/77 and 55/61 produced a usable spectrum. Sorting each set into Narrow/Medium/Wide by Δα still correlates with coarse-grained graph size at r = +0.69 (hMOF) and +0.72 (CoRE MOF), independently. Using the right graph fixes the graph; it does not fix the descriptor. Full nine-category breakdown on the Results page.
The two sets landed in the same place — which is a weaker fact than it sounds. The band, unconverged and computed on the wrong graph, was calibrated on computer-generated frameworks and applied unchanged to 61 experimentally synthesised MOFs from CoRE MOF 2019. The two sets landed at the same centre (Δα = 0.95–1.47 for the synthesised set against 1.02–1.41 for the hypothetical one; means differ by 0.018, p = 0.38) and the same spectrum shape. That does not show the descriptor transfers between datasets: it shows the same computational artefact was produced twice, on graphs of overlapping size, computed the same way. This replaced an earlier claim, made from four structures, that the two sets did not overlap; that claim was withdrawn on the Results page along with the reason it was wrong.
The pore-size correlation, also withdrawn. Δα correlates with pore diameter at r = −0.497, which looked like a finding. Controlling for the size of the analysed graph (its number of vertices, which were atoms) reduces it to r = +0.101, while graph size itself survives at +0.621, and adding pore size to a size-only model improves R² by only 0.005. The descriptor was largely measuring graph size, not pore geometry. We removed that claim rather than presenting it; the reasoning is set out in full in Results.

2Part I: The Pipeline, Stage by Stage

Six stages take a crystal structure file to a small set of numbers. Each stage depends only on the ones above it, and each is documented in full on the page it links to.

  1. What a MOF is, and why it matters

    A metal–organic framework is a crystal formed by joining metal clusters (nodes) to organic molecules (linkers), leaving a regular lattice of voids. The internal surface area of a gram of MOF can exceed a football pitch, which is why these materials are candidates for storing hydrogen, capturing CO₂, and separating gas mixtures.

    Establishes: what the material is, and why its porosity is worth engineering.

  2. Cutting a crystal into blocks

    A crystal structure file lists atom positions and a unit cell, and no bonds at all. Reasoning about the framework requires first deciding which atoms are bonded, then grouping them into building blocks: this metal cluster, that linker. This is the published metal-oxo decomposition, used here unmodified, and it is the step that turns a list of atoms into named parts.

    Establishes: what a node is, what a linker is, and how the program finds them without being told.

  3. Turning blocks into a graph — and getting periodicity right

    Collapsing every block to a point and every bond between blocks to a line turns the chemistry into a graph. The complication is that a crystal repeats forever: a bond may leave the unit cell on one side and re-enter on the other. Recording which bonds wrap around gives the correct graph; omitting it does not — UiO-66's metal cluster, which demonstrably connects to twelve linkers, is then reported as connecting to only one.

    Establishes: why the same crystal can produce two different graphs, and which one is correct.

  4. Measuring the graph

    Once the graph is correct, it becomes possible to ask which blocks hold the structure together — centrality. In a perfect crystal the answer is unhelpful: every metal cluster is an exact copy of every other, so none can be ranked above another. Removing one linker breaks that symmetry, and the measurement starts to discriminate. That failure of the naive approach is itself reported as a result.

    Establishes: what centrality measures, and why perfect symmetry defeats it.

  5. The multifractal spectrum

    Covering the graph with boxes of increasing radius and counting what falls in each gives a growth curve per block. A single growth rate would describe a uniform framework; a real framework is not uniform, so a range of growth rates is needed, and plotting how much of the structure follows each one traces an inverted parabola. Its width, Δα, was intended as a single number describing how varied the framework's local environments are — see the caveat above on whether that number is yet trustworthy.

    Establishes: what the parabola on every chart on this site is, and what its width was intended to mean.

  6. The band, the confounds, and what remains open

    Computing this width for 138 frameworks produces values that cluster into a narrow band. Most of the project's effort went into checking whether that band means anything: one apparent finding did not survive the check, one of our own headline claims did not survive a later check, and a third check — whether the band converges with supercell size at all — did not survive either.

    Establishes: what the project established, what it withdrew, and what remains unresolved.

What all six stages are in service of. Deciding whether one MOF is worth making takes hours of molecular simulation, and there are hundreds of thousands of candidates, so simulating all of them is not feasible. The aim here is not to replace that simulation. It is to compute something in seconds, from the crystal structure alone, that is informative enough to decide which few hundred are worth the hours. Every stage above was built to make that number trustworthy. Part I found that, as defined, it is not, and the verified graph pipeline was carried into Part II.
