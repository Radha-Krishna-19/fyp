From the papers we read to where the project is now: what was built from each, which code and notebooks produced which results, every mistake and its correction, and why each method was chosen over the alternatives.

2Stage 1. The question and the first two papers

When: August 2026

Purpose of this stepScreening MOFs for gas separation (xenon/krypton, then CO2) by simulation costs hours per framework. A number computed from connectivity alone, in seconds, could decide which frameworks are worth simulating. We read [1] because a MOF must first be cut into its chemical building units before any graph can be built, and [1] is the published, widely used rule for that. We read [2] because it gives a single curve (the spectrum) that summarises how a network is organised across all scales, which is what a fast screening number needs.
What we readBucior et al. 2019 [1] (MOFid / MOFkey and the metal-oxo decomposition: how to cut a MOF into metal clusters and organic linkers). Xiao, Wang and Li 2021 [2] (NMFA / iNMFA: the multifractal spectrum of a network). Song, Havlin and Makse 2005 [3] (box covering).
What we didAsked whether one number computed from a MOF's connectivity alone can decide which frameworks deserve hours of simulation. Translated the published decomposition into Python, built the building-block graph, and computed its multifractal spectrum width Δα.
Code and notebookscode/spectrum_pipeline/code_01 … code_07 (CIF reading, bond perception with Cordero covalent radii [10], metal-oxo decomposition, MOFid/MOFkey assembly), code_09 (network), code_10 (spectrum); first notebook on 8 frameworks.
What came outA working decomposition and a first spectrum for 8 frameworks.
Mistakes found here
  • Probabilities were averaged before taking logarithms, which made every spectrum more than ten times too narrow. (details)
How they were correctedBuild Z per trial, take ln Z, then average (commit of 31 Aug 2026).

Why this and not something else

Why the metal-oxo decomposition and not single-node or all-node?
It is the published MOFid rule [1] used to identify MOFs; it keeps each metal-oxo cluster as one node, which is the chemical unit that decides connectivity. The other two variants are implemented too (code_04b, code_04c) for comparison.
Why a multifractal spectrum and not simple graph statistics?
Degree, clustering and diameter describe one scale; the spectrum describes how mass is spread across all scales in one curve, which was the reason for testing it. Whether that adds anything over simple statistics is exactly what later stages tested.

3Stage 2. Matching the paper exactly, and the data we actually received

When: 1 September 2026

Purpose of this stepA result is only meaningful if it uses the published method exactly and if we know what data it was computed on. This stage made both true.
What we readRe-read [2] line by line; MOFX-DB [7]; hMOF [8]; CoRE MOF 2019 [9].
What we didCompared our formulas with the paper and rebuilt the band of Δα values on 77 frameworks fetched from MOFX-DB.
Code and notebooksnotebooks/spectrum/mof_band_analysis.ipynb; code/spectrum_pipeline/code_10_multifractal_spectrum.py.
What came outA band of 77 widths; a first claim that the width tracks pore size.
Mistakes found here
  • τ(q) had been fitted with a free intercept; the paper fits through the origin. No spectrum was valid (0 of 77). (details)
  • The influential nodes were analysed on their own subgraph, which has no edges in a MOF. (details)
  • We asked MOFX-DB for CoRE MOF 2019 (real crystals) and received hMOF (hypothetical, all Zn4O) without noticing.
  • The pore-size claim. (details)
How they were correctedτ = Σxy/Σx² (77 of 77 valid); cover the whole graph and read the measure at influential nodes; the dataset was relabelled as hMOF; the pore-size claim was retracted the same day.

Why this and not something else

Why follow the paper's through-origin fit?
Because τ(q) is defined that way in [2]; a free intercept changes the quantity, so results would not be comparable with the published method.

4Stage 3. Making it periodic and making it teachable

When: 2–7 September 2026

Purpose of this stepA crystal is infinite and periodic; a graph of one unit cell is not. Without periodicity the connectivity of real MOFs is wrong, so every later number would be wrong. The website and textbook exist so that a newcomer, the guide and the panel can check every step themselves.
What we readRCSR nets [5], Systre [4], the SBU concept [6]; HKUST-1, MOF-5, ZIF-8 and UiO-66 structure papers [11]–[14].
What we didBuilt the labelled quotient graph (every edge carries the lattice translation it crosses), checked it on the four demonstration MOFs (nets tbo, pcu, sod, fcu), and wrote the website, the textbook and the panel deck. Obtained 61 real CoRE MOF structures.
Code and notebookscode_09_network_analysis.py (build_periodic_block_graph), the browser engine website/mof_*.js (a second, independent implementation), notebooks/spectrum/real_mof_band.ipynb, docs/textbook/.
What came outCorrect coordination for all four nets (UiO-66 12-connected), the site, 61 real spectra.
Mistakes found here
  • Without the translation labels, UiO-66's cluster got the wrong number of neighbours. (details)
  • A claim built on four real structures.
How they were correctedQuotient graph with translations everywhere; the four-structure claim was withdrawn (7 Sep).

Why this and not something else

Why write a second implementation in JavaScript?
Two independent implementations from one specification catch each other's mistakes; the site also lets a reader compute every number in the browser.

5Stage 4. Is the number about the MOF or about the size of the graph?

When: 14–16 September 2026

Purpose of this stepBefore ranking MOFs by a number, the number must describe the MOF and not the size of the computer model. This stage tested exactly that, because a ranking built on graph size would be meaningless.
What we readFinite-size scaling practice (the spectrum must stop changing as the graph grows before it can be compared).
What we didSwept Δα over supercell sizes on four nets, re-ran the full bands on the correct graph (72 of 77 hMOF, 55 of 61 CoRE MOF), and proved that coarse-graining first or replicating first gives the identical graph.
Code and notebookscode_12_converged_band.py, code_13 … code_18, _run_size_scaling.py, _prove_ordering_equivalence.py; results in data/spectrum_descriptors/size_scaling/.
What came outΔα grows linearly in ln(diameter) on every net (R² ≥ 0.998, slope 0.69–0.89) and never converges; class tracks graph size (r = +0.69 hMOF, +0.72 CoRE MOF).
Mistakes found here
  • The Narrow/Medium/Wide classes were narrower than the error bar; two identical graphs fell in different classes. (details)
  • The published bands had been computed on the atomic graph while the text described the coarse-grained graph. (details)
  • Graphs were capped too small to measure any scaling. (details)
  • 48 of 76 hMOF structures were the same graph. (details)
  • The size effect itself: what looked like structure was graph size. (details)
How they were correctedBoth bands withdrawn (kept visible with reasons); a refusal rule for classes narrower than their error; coarse-grain first (48–675× cheaper, proved identical); a diversity guard on samples.

Why this and not something else

Why withdraw instead of tuning the method until a band appeared?
A number that changes with graph size cannot be compared between MOFs of different size; tuning parameters until it looks stable would hide that.

6Stage 5. The panel changes the question

When: after Panel Review 1

Purpose of this stepThe panel asked for several crystal shapes from one established unit cell. Shape matters for the original aim: it decides which faces are exposed to the gas, how far a molecule travels into the pores and how the crystals pack, so it changes adsorption in practice. BFDH was built first because it needs only the cell and the symmetry, so it works for any MOF.
What we readChen et al. 2020 [20] (one MOF chemistry, many crystal shapes), Hartman–Perdok theory [22], the Wulff construction [23], spglib [28].
What we didThe panel asked: from an established MOF's unit cell, can several crystal morphologies be generated, mathematically and computationally? Built the chain CIF → symmetry → allowed faces (systematic absences removed) → BFDH shape → shape descriptors.
Code and notebookscode/morphology/ (mofmorph/structure.py, faces.py, shape.py, models.py, run_phase1.py, 14 checks in tests.py); browser engine mof_morphology.js.
What came outBFDH: truncated octahedron for MOF-5, HKUST-1 and UiO-66; rhombic dodecahedron for ZIF-8. Cube, cuboctahedron and octahedron reached by the ratio R111/R100.
Mistakes found here
  • CSS colour tokens that referred to themselves blanked colours on every page.
How they were correctedFixed the tokens (9 Oct).

Why this and not something else

Why BFDH first?
It needs only the lattice and the space group, so it gives a shape for any CIF in seconds and is the standard first estimate in crystal-morphology work.
Why not jump to molecular dynamics of crystal growth?
Growth simulations of MOFs in solvent need force fields and time far beyond this project, and would not answer the mathematical question the panel asked.

7Stage 6. Proving it, deforming the cell, and the graph view of faces

When: October 2026

Purpose of this stepA picture of a shape is not a proof. The propositions show that the shapes follow mathematically from the unit cell. Changing the cell answers the second reading of the panel's question (one cell, controlled changes, many shapes). Counting the links each face cuts reuses the periodic graph of the first half for the second.
What we readWulff and BFDH theory, Hartman–Perdok F/S/K faces [22], Chen et al. link counting [20].
What we didWrote 16 propositions with proofs (each checked numerically). Changed the cell lengths and angles and showed which shapes follow (plates and rods). Counted, on the MOF's own periodic graph, how many links each face cuts, and ranked faces from that.
Code and notebooksmorph_math_page.js; mofmorph/deform.py, run_deform.py, tests_deform.py (11 checks); mofmorph/graphfaces.py, run_phase3.py, tests_phase3.py (11 checks).
What came outPropositions 1–16; plate, equant and rod shapes from cell deformation; attachment numbers pcu 1/2/3 (cube) and fcu 4/5/3 (octahedron, matching [20]).
Mistakes found hereNone recorded at this stage.
How they were corrected—

Why this and not something else

Why change the cell at all?
It is the second reading of the panel's question: one established cell, changed in a controlled way, gives a family of shapes with exact thresholds.

8Stage 7. Face energies from atoms

When: October 2026

Purpose of this stepBFDH uses geometry only and ignores chemistry. Real faces differ in how many bonds they break and how strong those bonds are, so an energy-based shape (Wulff) is needed as a second, independent prediction.
What we readUFF [29]; Wu and Jiang 2024 [21] (UFF workflow for MOFs).
What we didComputed the cleavage energy of every face from the atoms with a force field and built Wulff shapes.
Code and notebooksmofmorph/forcefield.py, run_phase4.py, tests_phase4.py (4 checks); results/phase4_results.json.
What came outUFF Wulff: cube for MOF-5, octahedron for UiO-66, rhombic dodecahedron for ZIF-8, truncated octahedron for HKUST-1.
Mistakes found here
  • HKUST-1 was first described as a cube with cut corners; it is a truncated octahedron. (details)
How they were correctedRead shapes from the computed face fractions only.

Why this and not something else

Why UFF and not DFT?
UFF covers every element in these MOFs and costs seconds per face; DFT on MOF slabs costs hours each. UFF is the baseline; a DFT spot check is planned (Kshitij, DFT_CHECK_PLAN.md).
Why unrelaxed energies?
Relaxation needs a reliable potential for MOF surfaces, which UFF is not; unrelaxed cleavage energies keep the comparison simple and are stated as such.

9Stage 8. The team's tasks: combine the methods, machine-learned energies, real crystals

When: 12–14 October 2026

Purpose of this stepEach teammate's task fed one question: which shape method agrees with real crystals? The blend tests whether geometry and energy together do better than either alone. CHGNet gives a second, machine-learned set of energies to compare with UFF. Rohith's dataset gives the real crystals to compare against, with the conditions they were grown under.
What we readCHGNet [Deng et al. 2023]; pymatgen slab generation; experimental morphology papers (Cravillon 2012 ZIF-8, Liu 2016 HKUST-1, Cubillas 2012 MOF-5, Umemura 2011, Butova 2023).
What we didCombined BFDH and Wulff into one rule with a weight w; wrote a Colab notebook for any CIF; post-processed Keerthi's CHGNet notebook runs (done by Rohith) for MOF-5, HKUST-1 and UiO-66; Rohith collected real crystal shapes with their growth conditions.
Code and notebooksmofmorph/hybrid.py, run_hybrid.py, MorphoGraph_hybrid.ipynb; mofmorph/chgnet_post.py, run_chgnet_cleavage.py, slab_check.py; data/experimental_morphology/.
What came outThe blend changes the shape only for MOF-5 and UiO-66. CHGNet slabs give only γtop+γbottom, too few equations for individual face energies; the cleavage energy (half the cheapest cut) gives shapes that disagree with experiment for MOF-5 and UiO-66.
Mistakes found here
  • The first notebook assumed every crystal is cubic and gave wrong shapes for others. (details)
  • A statement that CHGNet "will never" give a shape was too strong. (details)
  • Loose pieces in slabs were first read as split metal clusters; they were linker fragments. (details)
How they were correctedA general version for any crystal system; the cleavage-energy workaround with its limits; the slab pieces identified.

Why this and not something else

Why combine BFDH and Wulff?
BFDH uses geometry only and Wulff uses energy only; a blend tests whether both carry information. With four MOFs no best weight can be chosen without fitting the test set, so none is claimed.
Why the cleavage energy and not separate face energies?
The slabs give one equation per cut with two unknown faces; the cleavage energy is the standard, well-defined quantity those slabs do determine.

10Stage 9. An independent review of the code and the chemistry

When: 14–15 October 2026

Purpose of this stepWork checked only by its author is not checked. A second reviewer re-ran every script, tested the chemistry, and looked for weak points before the guide review.
What we readThe 35 open-access papers behind the experimental dataset; CoRE MOF 2019 [9].
What we didA second reviewer re-ran and checked everything, built the experimental dataset (134 records, 35 papers, 12 MOFs), confirmed from two primary papers that the UiO-66 octahedra are grown with modulators, tested the force field's sensitivity, and ran the pipeline on 8 CoRE MOFs of six crystal systems.
Code and notebookstests_review.py (the review checks), run_bond_sensitivity.py, run_core.py, slab_options_check.py, tests_review.py, dataset/build_dataset.py.
What came outThe Experiment page: every method scored against additive-free crystals.
Mistakes found here
  • Hydrogen atoms were dropped from the ZIF-8 energy cell ({111}: 0.312 instead of 0.619 J/m²). (details)
  • The MOF-5 cube had been trusted because it matched experiment; it depends on the flat bond cost. (details)
  • One "correct" shape per MOF, while real shapes depend on solvent, additive and time. (details)
  • The hybrid weight could be tuned to the four test MOFs. (details)
How they were correctedEnergies rebuilt with all atoms; the MOF-5 cube quoted only with its caveat; conditions recorded for every record; the weight shown fixed at w = 0.5.

Why this and not something else

Why keep only additive-free crystals in the benchmark?
No model here contains a solvent or modulator, so comparing with crystals grown with additives would test something the models do not represent.

11Stage 10. Joining the two halves

When: 16 October 2026

Purpose of this stepThe first half should not be wasted if it can be used. This stage tested, with a notebook that Radha Krishna ran, whether the spectrum of the first half carries any information about crystal shape, and so whether the two halves form one project.
What we readOur own first-half results.
What we didAsked whether the first-half spectrum is related to the second-half shapes. Built finite crystal particles (no wrap-around) of chosen shapes and measured Δα; compared Δα with predicted BFDH shapes on 55 CoRE MOFs. The notebook was run by Radha Krishna in Google Colab; all 15 checks passed.
Code and notebookscode/spectrum_shape_relation/ (Spectrum_Shape_Relation.ipynb and the scripts it calls), code_19_spectrum_vs_shape.py.
What came outΔα of a finite MOF-5 particle separates flat and long particles from compact ones, but not compact shapes from each other; across 55 CoRE MOFs the periodic Δα carries no shape information once graph size is held fixed.
Mistakes found hereNone recorded at this stage.
How they were corrected—

Why this and not something else

Why compare at equal block count and not equal diameter?
In a pcu net graph distance behaves like the taxicab distance, so an octahedron has a much smaller diameter than a cube with the same number of blocks; equal block count compares the same amount of crystal.
Why Spearman and a size-controlled (partial) correlation?
Spearman is robust to outliers and non-linear links; controlling size was necessary because size had produced every earlier false result.

13Stage 12. The dimension profile of a crystal particle

When: 25 October 2026

Purpose of this stepStage 10 left one question open: the spectrum width separates plates and rods but not the compact shapes the methods predict. The Fractals and Scaling course suggested keeping the whole scaling curve instead of one number.
What we readSong, Havlin and Makse, Nature 433, 392 (2005) (cluster growing); the Fractals and Scaling course (Santa Fe Institute), units on box counting and scaling.
What we didMeasured the mass–radius curve M(r) and the local dimension d(r) of MOF-5 boxes of about 1000 cells and of the seven polyhedra of stage 10 at two sizes, and calibrated the method on a path, a square grid and a cubic grid.
Code and notebookscode/spectrum_shape_relation/run_04_dimension_profile_of_crystal_particles.py, six new checks in check_spectrum_shape_relation.py, step 4 of Spectrum_Shape_Relation.ipynb.
What came outCube, plate and rod have the local dimension of a 3D, 2D and (nearly) 1D lattice; r½/D separates the cube, truncated cube and truncated octahedron predicted for MOF-5 (Spectrum ↔ Shape, Test 4).
Mistakes found here
  • The first draft explained the local dimension staying below 3 by the linkers, which add a step without branching. The cubic grid, which has no linkers, also peaks at 2.64, so the cause is the finite size of the graph and the lattice itself.
How they were correctedReference lattices were added to the calculation, and particles are compared with them rather than with the ideal 1, 2 and 3.

Why this and not something else

Why calibrate on lattices of known dimension?
On a finite graph d(r) never reaches its ideal value; only a reference computed the same way shows what a 1D, 2D or 3D object gives at that size.
Does this predict crystal shape?
No. It identifies a shape from a particle graph; which shape a MOF grows still comes from the morphology models.

16Defending Each Key Decision

17Where We Are Now

  • Answered: the panel's question. From one established unit cell the project generates several morphologies, by growth rates (BFDH, graph rule), by energies (Wulff with UFF or CHGNet) and by changing the cell, with the mathematics proved and checked.
  • Not yet answered: which method predicts real crystals best. Only MOF-5 separates the methods, the force-field cube depends on one constant, and the machine-learned energies are unvalidated for MOF surfaces.
  • Carried over from the first half: the periodic quotient graph (used for face counting) and the habit of controlling size. The spectrum itself responds only to flat or long particles, not to compact crystal shapes.
