From a CIF File to a Crystal Shape

Every step from the numbers in a CIF file to the crystal shape, computed in your browser with the full working shown: lattice, metric tensor, centring and point group found from the atoms, structure factors, face spacings, the BFDH rule, the shape built plane by plane, and the map of every shape a cubic crystal can take.

What This Page Does

The panel asked whether a computer can start from the unit cell of an established MOF and produce the crystal shapes it can take. This page does the first, purely geometric version of that, in front of you: it reads a CIF file and computes every step from the six cell numbers to the finished shape, printing each intermediate number so the working can be checked by hand.

Any Lattice: Rods, Plates and Compact Crystals

The numbered steps below treat cubic crystals. A cubic cell is the same along all three axes, so BFDH can only give compact shapes (cube, octahedron and the forms between them). A cell with one axis much shorter or longer than the others, or with symmetry that cancels the planes along one axis, can give a rod (needle) or a plate. This section runs the same rule on a file of any lattice: hexagonal, trigonal, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic or triclinic. It finds the symmetry from the atoms, works out which planes cancel, builds the crystal from its 40 widest plane families, and measures how long or flat the result is.

For each plane family {hkl}: 1/dhkl = |ha* + kb* + lc*|, from the metric of any cell. The family is absent at order n if some symmetry operation (R, t) has nhR = nh and nh·t not a whole number; deff = dhkl/n for the first order that survives. BFDH puts the faces of each family at distance R = deff,max/deff from the centre. The shape is then measured by its gyration tensor: principal radii a ≥ b ≥ c, and Zingg's classes: compact if b/a ≥ 2/3 and c/b ≥ 2/3, rod if only b/a < 2/3, plate if only c/b < 2/3, lath if both.

2Read the File

A CIF is a plain text file. Two things in it matter here: the box that repeats to fill space (the unit cell, given by three edge lengths and three angles) and the list of atoms inside it. Each atom is given as an element and three fractional coordinates: how far along each edge of the box it sits, as a fraction of that edge.

Unit cell: a, b, c (Å) and α (between b and c), β (between a and c), γ (between a and b). An atom at fractional coordinates x = (x, y, z) sits at the point r = xa + yb + zc, and also at r + ua + vb + wc for every whole number u, v, w.

3The Lattice Vectors and the Metric Tensor

The six numbers are turned into three arrows, the cell edges a, b, c, written in ordinary x-y-z coordinates. From the arrows we build one 3×3 table, the metric tensor G, which holds every length and angle of the cell. Every later step that needs a distance or an angle reads it from G. G does not change when the crystal is turned, so it describes the lattice itself, not the way it happens to be drawn.

a = (a, 0, 0),  b = (b cosγ, b sinγ, 0),  c = (c cosβ, c(cosα − cosβ cosγ)/sinγ, cz), with cz fixed by |c| = c.

Put the three edges as the rows of a matrix A. Then G = A AT, that is Gij = ai·aj: the diagonal holds a², b², c² and the off-diagonal entries ab cosγ, ac cosβ, bc cosα.

Cell volume V = √(det G). The length of any lattice vector with integer or fractional components u is |u|² = uT G u.

4Is the Lattice Cubic? The Conventional Cell

Files often store the smallest box that repeats, not the most symmetric one. A face-centred cube, for example, can be stored as a slanted box with 60° angles that is one quarter of the cube. To see the cube we look for whole-number combinations of the stored edges that are equal in length and at right angles.

The lattice is cubic exactly when there is an integer matrix M (its rows say how to combine the stored edges) such that M G MT = ac² I. The new edges M A form the conventional cube of edge ac, which contains |det M| lattice points. Three candidates cover every cubic lattice: M = I (the file already holds the cube); the 60° rhombohedral cell of a face-centred cube (det M = 4); the 109.47° rhombohedral cell of a body-centred cube (det M = 2). Agreement is tested to a relative tolerance of 2×10−4, which allows for the rounding of the numbers in the file.

5Lattice Centring, Found from the Atoms

A cube of lattice points may have extra points that repeat the whole pattern: one at the centre of the body (a body-centred cube, I) or one at the centre of each face (a face-centred cube, F). We test this directly. Shift every atom by half a cell along a diagonal; if each one lands on an atom of the same element, that shift is a symmetry of the crystal.

A translation t is a symmetry when, for every atom j, some atom k of the same element satisfies |(xj + t − xk − m) Ac| < 0.02 Å for some whole-number vector m (the wrap-around into the next cell). I-centring is t = (½, ½, ½); F-centring is the three vectors (0, ½, ½), (½, 0, ½), (½, ½, 0). The test is run with all atoms and again without hydrogen, because hydrogen positions in a CIF are often placed by software and can break a symmetry the framework has.

6The Point Group, Found from the Atoms

Now we turn and reflect the crystal instead of shifting it. If the crystal looks exactly the same after a rotation (perhaps combined with a shift), that rotation is a symmetry. Which rotations survive decides which crystal faces are equivalent, and so which faces must grow at the same rate.

An operation (R, t) is a symmetry when every atom j is sent onto an atom of the same element: Rxj + t ≡ xk (mod 1, within 0.02 Å). The shift t is not known in advance. However, a symmetry must send the first atom of the rarest element onto some atom of that element, so only that many values of t need to be tried. Four operations decide the cubic point group: inversion, a 3-fold axis along the body diagonal, a 4-fold axis along an edge, and a 4-fold rotoinversion along an edge.

7Which Planes Cancel: Structure Factors and the Space Group

A crystal face is a plane of atoms, and the face grows by adding the next identical layer. If the centring places an identical layer exactly half-way between two planes of a family, the true repeat distance of that family is half its nominal spacing. X-ray diffraction shows this directly: the waves scattered by the two layers are exactly out of step and cancel, so that reflection is missing. We compute this cancellation from the atoms in the file.

Structure factor: F(hkl) = Σj Zj exp(2πi (hxj + kyj + lzj)), taking each atom's atomic number Zj as its weight.

If the translations t1, …, tm are symmetries, the atoms come in sets {x, x + t1, …}, and the sum factorises as F(h) = S(h) × Σone atom per set Zj exp(2πi h·xj), with S(h) = Σs exp(2πi h·ts). When S(h) = 0, the reflection vanishes whatever the atoms are. This is a systematic absence. The weights change the size of an allowed reflection but never make an absent one non-zero.

Glide planes (a reflection combined with a half-cell shift) forbid further reflections. Each possible glide group is checked against the one reflection it would remove.

8Face Families, Spacings and Effective Spacings

Every set of parallel lattice planes is a possible crystal face, labelled by three whole numbers (hkl), its Miller indices. Planes related by the crystal's symmetry form a family, written {hkl}, and all members of a family behave identically. For each family we need one number, the distance between neighbouring identical layers. The picture shows why the centring can halve it.

Spacing: 1/d² = hT G−1 h. For the cubic cell G = a²I, so dhkl = a / √(h² + k² + l²).

First allowed order: the smallest n for which n(hkl) is not absent (Step 6). Effective spacing deff = dhkl / n.

The members of a family are all signed permutations of (hkl) in point group m-3m. In -43m a family with no zero index splits into two halves of equal size, told apart by the sign of hkl. Every primitive (hkl) with indices up to 6 is listed.

9How Far Out Each Face Sits: the BFDH Rule

A face made of widely spaced planes is densely packed with atoms and is flat at the molecular scale, so new material sticks to it slowly. A slow face moves outward slowly and stays large on the finished crystal, while fast faces grow themselves out of existence. The simplest version of this idea says that each face's distance from the centre is inversely proportional to its effective spacing.

BFDH rule (Bravais 1866, Friedel 1907, Donnay and Harker 1937): the distance of face family f from the centre is Rf ∝ 1/deff,f. Scale it so the widest-spaced family is at distance 1: Rf = deff,max / deff,f. Only ratios of distances matter, because the shape does not depend on its size.

10Building the Shape, Plane by Plane

Every member of every family becomes a flat wall at its distance R from the centre, facing outward. The crystal is everything that lies inside all the walls. We build it as a sculptor would: start from a large block and cut it with one wall at a time, nearest walls first. Most walls end up outside the shape and cut nothing. The animation shows only the cuts that change it.

The shape is the convex polytope P = {x : np·x ≤ Rp for every plane p}, with outward unit normal n = h/|h|. In a cubic cell the normal of (hkl) points along (h, k, l), and the wall is the plane hx + ky + lz = R|h|.

One cut: every face polygon is clipped to the inner side of the new plane (each edge that crosses the plane is cut at the crossing point A + (B − A) sA/(sA − sB), with s = n·x − R). The crossing points, ordered around their centre, form the new face.

A corner of the shape is a point on three planes with independent normals: the 3×3 linear system Nx = r, solved by Cramer's rule.

A plane cuts only if some corner of the current shape lies farther out than R in that direction. Each cut is first shown from the side it is made on. 11Which Faces Survive, and by How Much the Others Miss

For each family we compare two distances: how far out its wall sits, and how far the finished shape reaches in that direction. If the wall is farther out than the shape reaches, it never touches the crystal and that face does not appear.

Reach of the shape along a direction n (the support function): hP(n) = max over the corners v of n·v. On the finished shape every wall satisfies Rf ≥ hP(nf). A family carries faces when equality holds and the wall meets the shape in a polygon of positive area; it is absent when Rf > hP(nf), and the difference is the margin by which it misses. When Rf = hP(nf) but the face has zero area, the wall touches the shape only at a corner or along an edge.

12Measuring and Naming the Shape

The finished shape is reduced to a few numbers: how much of its surface each family covers, its volume, and how close it is to a sphere. The name follows from which families are present and from the outline of their faces.

Area of a face with corners v1, …, vm in order: A = ½ |Σt=2m−1 (vt − v1) × (vt+1 − v1)|.

Volume: the shape is a union of pyramids from the centre to each face, of height Rf, so V = ⅓ Σf Rf Af.

Sphericity: Ψ = π1/3 (6V)2/3 / A, the area of the sphere of the same volume divided by the actual area (1 for a sphere).

13Beyond BFDH: Every Shape a Cubic Crystal Can Take

BFDH fixes one set of distances from the geometry alone. Real growth conditions (solvent, temperature, added modulators) change how fast each face grows, which moves the distances. For a cubic crystal bounded by {100}, {110} and {111}, two ratios decide the shape completely, so all possible shapes fit on one map. Click or drag on the map to see the shape at any point. The diamond marks where BFDH puts this file.

Set R100 = 1, x = R110/R100, y = R111/R100. Each boundary follows from the reach of one simple shape (Step 10):

  • Cube (six {100} walls at 1, corners (±1, ±1, ±1)): its reach along (111)/√3 is 3/√3 = √3, and along (110)/√2 it is 2/√2 = √2. No other face cuts it when y ≥ √3 and x ≥ √2.
  • Octahedron (eight {111} walls at y, corners (±√3 y, 0, 0) and permutations): its reach along (100) is √3 y, and along (110)/√2 it reaches the edge midpoint (√3y/2, √3y/2, 0), which gives √(3/2) y. It is pure when y ≤ 1/√3 and x ≥ √(3/2) y.
  • Rhombic dodecahedron (twelve {110} walls at x, corners (±√2 x, 0, 0) and (±x/√2)(1, 1, 1)): its reach along (100) is √2 x and along (111)/√3 it is √(3/2) x. It is pure when x ≤ 1/√2 and y ≥ √(3/2) x.
  • Cuboctahedron: the {111} triangles and {100} squares share corners at (1, 1, 0), where (1,1,1)·(1,1,0)/√3 = 2/√3. So it occurs only on the line y = 2/√3, and only for x ≥ √2. Above the line the shape is a truncated cube, below it a truncated octahedron.

The map is coloured by the area fraction of each family (blue {100}, red {111}, green {110}); the black lines are the exact boundaries above. It treats the two halves of {111} as equal. The tetrahedral shapes that -43m allows when they differ are on the Morphology page.

14What This Shows, and What It Does Not

  • Steps 1 to 7 are exact crystallography of the file. The cell, the cubic lattice, the centring, the point group, the space group and the effective spacings follow from the numbers in the CIF, and for the four bundled files they agree with spglib.
  • Step 8 is a rule, not a physical model of growth. BFDH ignores the chemistry of the faces, the solvent, the temperature and any modulator. Step 8 also shows that the BFDH shape depends only on the centring and the point group, so MOF-5, HKUST-1 and UiO-66, all face-centred cubic, receive the same truncated octahedron. Laboratories grow these three MOFs in different shapes, so BFDH cannot by itself tell them apart. How the project's other models (graph attachment numbers, force-field and CHGNet surface energies) move each MOF to a different point of the map in Step 12, and how those points compare with laboratory crystals, is on the Morphology, Surface Energy and Experiment pages.
  • Only equilibrium-like shapes are produced. Every shape here is a convex polytope with flat faces. A rod or a plate appears only when the cell itself is elongated or flattened, or its symmetry cancels the planes along one axis (the "Any Lattice" section). Spheres, and rods or plates grown under kinetic control or with additives that block particular faces, are not produced by this construction.
  • The numbered steps are for cubic crystals. A file whose lattice is not cubic stops at Step 3 with a message; the "Any Lattice" section near the top of the page handles it.
