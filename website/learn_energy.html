2Surface Energy and Cleavage Energy

The idea. Inside a crystal every building unit is held by its neighbours on all sides. At a surface some of those bonds and attractions are missing, so a surface costs energy. The surface energy γ is that cost per unit area, in joules per square metre (J/m²). A face with a low γ is cheap, so a crystal shows a lot of it.

Cutting makes two faces. If you split a crystal along a plane, you get two new surfaces at once, one on each piece. So the energy of the cut, divided by its area, is the sum of two surface energies:

Ecut / A = γtop + γbottom

Half of the cheapest such cut is called the cleavage energy. If the two pieces look the same (the crystal has a centre of inversion, as MOF-5 and HKUST-1 do), the cleavage energy is simply the surface energy. If they differ, the cleavage energy is an average of the two, so it can only be equal to or higher than the cheaper face: it is an upper bound, never an underestimate.

Numbers in this project. MOF faces are cheap compared with metals (metals are roughly 1–2 J/m²): MOF-5 {100} is 0.148 J/m² with the force field and 0.127 J/m² from CHGNet, because a MOF is mostly empty space and only a few bonds cross any plane.

3Terminations and Slabs

  • Termination. One face direction (hkl) can be cut at many depths, and each depth leaves different groups on the surface. A cut between layers that breaks only linkers is cheap; a cut through the metal clusters is expensive. The crystal shows the cheapest termination.
  • Slab. To compute a face's energy, programs build a slab: a layer of crystal a few nanometres thick that repeats sideways but has vacuum above and below, so it has exactly two faces. Its energy minus the energy of the same atoms in the bulk crystal gives γtop + γbottom.
  • Vacuum gap. The empty space (20 Å in the CHGNet runs) stops the top face from feeling the next copy's bottom face.
  • Stoichiometric slab. It has the same chemical formula as the bulk, so "slab minus bulk" compares like with like. The CHGNet results use only such slabs.
  • Symmetric slab. Same termination on both sides, so γ = half the slab's excess energy exactly. For these MOFs the symmetric slabs that exist are expensive terminations (1.4 to 9 times the cheapest bound), so they do not help (Experiment, section 5).
  • Thickness. A slab must be thick enough that its middle looks like bulk crystal. The CHGNet slabs are 15–30 Å (one or two framework layers), which is thin; this is one of the stated limits.

Units. Programs report energies in electron-volts (eV) per square ångström. 1 eV/Å² = 16.02 J/m². Force fields often use kcal/mol per bond (the flat bond cost in Phase 4 is 70 kcal/mol, about 3 eV).

4Relaxation

When a surface is made, the atoms near it are no longer pulled equally from all sides, so they move a little to a lower-energy position. Letting them move is called relaxation; it is done by moving the atoms along the forces on them until the forces are almost zero (the CHGNet results use only slabs whose largest force fell below 0.05 eV/Å). A relaxed surface energy is lower than an unrelaxed one. Our force-field energies (Phase 4) are unrelaxed: the atoms stay where they are in the bulk. The CHGNet energies are relaxed.

5Where Energies Come From: Three Levels

Every energy above must be calculated from the atoms. There are three common ways, from cheap and rough to expensive and accurate:

Force field (UFF)A fixed formula for each pair of atoms: a cost for every bond plus a Lennard-Jones attraction and repulsion between atoms that are not bonded. UFF has parameters for every element.SecondsPhase 4 energies for every face (Surface Energy)
Machine-learned potential (CHGNet)A neural network trained on many quantum calculations; it predicts energies and forces from atom positions.Minutes to hours on a GPUKeerthi's slab notebook (Experiment)

Lennard-Jones in one line. Two atoms at distance r attract weakly when far apart and repel strongly when too close; E(r) = D[(x/r)12 − 2(x/r)6], lowest (−D) at r = x. In MOFs this part is small; most of the surface cost comes from bonds cut.

Why all three. The cheap method gives every face of every MOF; the middle one is a second, independent estimate; the expensive one decides when the two disagree, as they do for MOF-5 and UiO-66.

6From Energies to Real Crystals

  • Equilibrium shape (Wulff). If a crystal had time to reach its lowest total surface energy, the face distances would be proportional to their γ (Learn, section 8).
  • Growth shape (BFDH, attachment). Real crystals usually stop growing before equilibrium; then the slowest-growing faces win.
  • Solvent and modulators. Molecules in the solution stick to some faces more than others and slow them down, which changes the shape. A modulator (for example acetic or benzoic acid) is added on purpose to do this. None of our models contains a solvent or modulator, so we compare them only with crystals grown without additives.

7Combining Two Methods, and the Risk of Overfitting

BFDH gives one distance RB for each face, Wulff gives another, RW. A blend with a dial w between 0 and 1 is R = RB1−w · RWw (a weighted geometric mean: w = 0 is pure BFDH, w = 1 pure Wulff).

Overfitting. A dial can always be turned until the answer matches the examples you test it on. With four MOFs, the "best" w would just be the one that suits those four, and would say nothing about a fifth. The honest test is a hold-out: choose w on some MOFs, then check it on others that were not used. Until that is done the site shows w = 0.5 and does not rank it (Hybrid).

8The Statistics We Use

  • Spearman correlation ρ. Rank both lists from smallest to largest and correlate the ranks. ρ = +1 means "always goes up together", 0 means "no relation", −1 "one up, the other down". Ranks make it robust to outliers and to curved relations.
  • p-value by permutation. Shuffle one list at random 10 000 times and count how often a shuffled ρ is as large as the real one. That fraction is p. A small p (below 0.05) means the relation is unlikely to be chance.
  • Bootstrap interval. Re-draw the data with replacement 5 000 times, recompute ρ each time, and keep the middle 95% of the values. That range shows how much ρ could move with different data.

9Periodic Tiling Versus a Finite Particle

  • Periodic tiling (torus). The spectrum work joined each edge of the supercell to the opposite edge, so the graph has no surface at all. That is right for "the infinite crystal", but it has no shape.
  • Finite particle. Cutting the graph out without wrap-around gives a particle with a surface, so it can be a cube, a plate or a rod (Spectrum ↔ Shape).
  • Graph diameter and taxicab distance. In a grid-like net such as MOF-5's pcu, the number of steps between two blocks is the sum of the steps along each axis (like a taxi on city blocks). So an octahedron, the "taxicab ball", has a much smaller diameter than a cube with the same number of blocks. That is why shapes are compared at equal block count, not equal diameter.

10Why Hydrogen Atoms Can Hide a Crystal's Symmetry

X-ray experiments see hydrogen atoms poorly, so their positions in a CIF file are often placed by a program and are slightly off, or disordered. Symmetry-finding software (spglib) then finds almost no symmetry. ZIF-8 is the example: with its hydrogens the file looks like P1 (no symmetry), without them it is the true cubic I-43m. The project finds the symmetry without hydrogens but always keeps every atom, hydrogens included, when it calculates energies (that was the ZIF-8 bug on the Mistakes page).
