In simple words

Before the panel we described each MOF by one number from how its building blocks are connected. After the panel we predicted the outside shape of its crystals. Here we ask three questions. If we build small MOF crystals of different shapes, does that number change? Does it change between the shapes our different methods predict? And across many real MOFs, does the number tell us anything about the predicted shape?

In technical words

Every earlier spectrum was measured on a torus (the labelled quotient graph tiled with wrap-around), which has no surface and no shape. Here the quotient graph is tiled without wrap-around, every block is placed at its Cartesian centroid, and the graph is cut to a finite particle: a box of unit cells, or a polyhedron {x : nf·x ≤ s Rf} with the face distances Rf of a morphology prediction. Δα uses the settings of the full bands: top 20% of blocks by degree as influential, radii to half the diameter, 20 trials, seeds 0–4. Error bars are the seed spread only.

0Test 4: The Dimension Profile of a Crystal Particle (MOF-5)

Why this test. Tests 1 to 3 compress the whole scaling behaviour of a particle into one number, Δα. This test keeps the whole curve. It uses the mass–radius form of box counting (cluster growing; Song, Havlin and Makse, Nature 433, 392, 2005), the method of the Fractals and Scaling course: M(r) is the average number of blocks within graph distance r of a block, and the local dimension is d(r) = d ln M / d ln r. An object that is three-, two- or one-dimensional at a given scale has d(r) near 3, 2 or 1 at that scale.

Method. The particles are the MOF-5 boxes of Test 1 enlarged to about 1000 unit cells (cube 10×10×10, slab 16×16×4, plate 32×32×1, rod 64×4×4), and the seven polyhedra of Test 2 at about 4000 and 8000 blocks. Distances are computed from 64 random centre blocks by breadth-first search, with five seeds. Two read-outs: d at the half-mass radius r½ (the radius that holds half the particle), and r½/D, where D is the largest distance found. A finite graph never reaches the ideal 1, 2 and 3, so the same calculation is done on three lattices of known dimension, and the particles are compared with them.

Result 1: plates and rods are lower-dimensional objects, quantitatively. At the half-mass radius the MOF-5 cube gives d = 1.80 against 1.78 for the cubic lattice, the plate 1.41 against 1.40 for the square lattice, and the rod 0.94 against 0.83 for the path; the slab lies between (1.55). The left panel shows where each particle stops being three-dimensional: the plate, one cell thick, follows the square lattice almost from the start; the rod, four cells wide, is three-dimensional at short range and approaches the path beyond about a third of its diameter. This is consistent with Test 1: the spectrum is fitted over radii up to half the diameter, where the plate and the rod have a lower local dimension than the cube.

Result 2: the fill profile separates the compact shapes that Δα could not. r½/D is 0.37 for the cube, 0.44 for the rhombic dodecahedron and 0.56 for the octahedron (about 4000 blocks). The three different MOF-5 predictions fall in that order at both sizes: UFF Wulff (cube) 0.37 and 0.36, the blend (truncated cube) 0.42 and 0.46, BFDH (truncated octahedron) 0.51 and 0.51. Each gap is at least 3.6 times the largest seed spread. CHGNet, which also predicts a truncated octahedron, gives 0.54 and 0.52, close to BFDH. The reason is geometric: in the pcu net graph distance behaves like the taxicab distance, in which a cube fills its own diameter quickly and an octahedron (the taxicab ball) keeps its blocks close to the centre.

The dimension profile identifies the shape of a particle from its graph alone; it does not predict which shape a MOF grows, and it adds no evidence for or against any morphology model. Its value for the project is a graph descriptor that is sensitive to crystal shape, computed with the same machinery as the spectrum, for use on particles produced by any method (a growth simulation, a generative model). Limits: one MOF; one rule for building a particle (block centroid inside the polyhedron, so surfaces are a staircase one block high; four of the fourteen polyhedra leave detached blocks at their corners (at most 2.5% of the blocks, the octahedron at about 4000), and only the largest connected piece is used); r½ is an integer radius, so r½/D moves in steps of 1/D (about 0.02); D is the largest distance found from the sampled centres, a lower bound on the true diameter.

0What Carries Over From the First Half

QuestionAnswerEvidence
Does Δα of a finite particle depend on how flat or long it is?Yes, for MOF-5, beyond sizeTest 1
Does it separate compact shapes (cube vs octahedron vs truncated octahedron)?No; differences are within the method's own scatterTest 2
Does the periodic Δα predict a MOF's crystal shape?No, once graph size is controlledTest 3
Does the mass–radius curve of a finite particle carry shape information?Yes: dimensionality (cube, plate, rod) and the compact shapes (cube, truncated cube, truncated octahedron)Test 4

What carries over is the graph (the labelled quotient graph, used by the morphology work in Phase 3) and the habit of controlling size. The spectrum width itself becomes useful for shape only when it is measured on a finite particle, and then only for anisotropy (plate, rod). The full mass–radius curve (Test 4) carries more: it also separates the compact shapes. The next tests are the cell-deformation shapes of Cell Deform, which are plates and rods, and a second MOF with a connected block graph.

Tests 1, 2 and 4 use one structure (MOF-5): it is the only demonstration MOF whose file holds an orthogonal conventional cell and whose block graph is connected. A block belongs to a particle if its centroid is inside the shape, so surfaces are a staircase one block high. BFDH shape names for low-symmetry crystals use cubic-style labels and are not used in any statistic; only sphericity and aspect ratio are.
