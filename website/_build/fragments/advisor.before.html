0What This Page Is For

A MOF powder is a heap of small crystals, and a gas enters each crystal only through its surface. Which faces form that surface, and how much surface the crystal has for its volume, depend on the crystal's shape. Two batches of the same MOF with different shapes can therefore take up gas at different rates even though their pores are identical. Before a chemist spends weeks adjusting a synthesis, it helps to know three things: which shape the structure tends to give on its own, how hard it is to push it to another shape, and whether that other shape would expose more open pores. This page puts the project's answers to those three questions for each MOF in one place. Every number on it is read from a result file named under Sources.

0One MOF at a Time

Choose a MOF. The shape is drawn with the same engines as the other pages: a Wulff shape from structure-only surface energies for cubic MOFs, and the BFDH shape for other lattices, where no energy model is available here. Drag to turn it; click a face for its share of the surface and how open it is to gas. A star marks a MOF scored with a close stand-in structure.

Steering the Shape

An additive that binds one face family lowers that family's surface energy, and a face with lower energy grows slower and takes more of the surface. The table gives, for each face, how far its energy must fall before it becomes the main face and before the crystal becomes that face's pure shape. Below the table are the additive types in the laboratory records that gave each face for this MOF, with the papers. Small numbers mean a mild additive should be enough; large numbers mean strong, face-selective binding is needed.

What Each Shape Exposes to a Gas

Each face is cut at its lowest-energy termination, and every point of that plane is tested for whether a CO2 or N2 molecule (probe radius 1.65 or 1.82 Å) can sit there without overlapping a framework atom. The open fraction is the share of the face a molecule can enter through. For a whole crystal, the open surface is the area-weighted open fraction of its faces, and the diffusion length is the crystal's volume divided by its surface area, L = V/A, the usual characteristic length for diffusion into a particle (one third of the radius for a sphere). It is quoted for a crystal of unit volume, so a rounder shape, with less surface for the same volume, has a longer L. A shorter diffusion length means a crystal fills faster (fill time grows with its square); the initial uptake rate grows with the open surface per unit volume (open fraction times A/V). These are geometric indicators of access, not computed uptakes: no adsorption isotherm or diffusion coefficient is calculated here.

0How Good Is the Prediction?

The predicted main face was compared with the dominant face reported in each laboratory record of the laboratory dataset (Against experiment) for which the structure file exists. Two structure-only models are scored against two baselines that use the laboratory data: the most common face for that MOF in the other papers (each paper held out in turn), and the most common face among the other MOFs (the whole MOF held out, which is the situation of a new MOF nobody has grown yet).

How to read this. On records without an additive, the structure-only Wulff model gets the main face right exactly as often as the most common laboratory answer for that MOF, and it needs no laboratory data; for a MOF that has never been grown, the laboratory baseline (whole MOF held out) is right far less often. BFDH alone is weaker than the Wulff model on these records. On records with an additive, every model is right in fewer than half of the cases and their intervals overlap, so none of them predicts the effect of an additive; BFDH happens to score highest there. The figures in brackets are 95 % Wilson intervals: with 32 and 44 records, differences of a few records are not significant.

0All 138 Dataset Structures

The structure-only steps (symmetry, allowed faces, BFDH shape, overall form) were also run on all 138 CIF files of the first half of the project. These are unvalidated predictions: no laboratory shape is known for these structures.

Most hMOF files are stored without symmetry (space group P1), so the symmetry-based steps see only a triclinic cell and every hMOF structure comes out compact. This says more about how the hypothetical files were written than about the frameworks.

0Limits

  • The surface energies are unrelaxed UFF values without solvent or electrostatics. They rank faces; they are not absolute energies, and the steering percentages are relative indications, not additive binding energies.
  • The IRMOF-8 file keeps only P21/c symmetry because of how its naphthalene linkers are ordered, so its faces are not those of an ideal Fm-3m framework.
  • ZIF-67 is built from ZIF-8 by replacing zinc with cobalt; MIL-53(Fe) and UiO-66-NH2 are scored with stand-in structures and reported separately. SNU-70 and Zn4O(FMA)3 have no structure file here and are not predicted.
  • Mg-MOF-74 has a hexagonal lattice (space group R-3); only the BFDH shape is drawn, so its steering is not computed.
  • Plates, spheres and intergrown crystals come from growth kinetics and are outside every equilibrium shape model used here.
