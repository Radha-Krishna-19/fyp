0What This Page Is For

A MOF powder is a heap of small crystals, and a gas enters each crystal only through its surface. Which faces form that surface, and how much surface the crystal has for its volume, depend on the crystal's shape. Two batches of the same MOF with different shapes can therefore take up gas at different rates even though their pores are identical. Before a chemist spends weeks adjusting a synthesis, three answers help. Which shape does the structure tend to give on its own? How hard is it to push it to another shape? Would that other shape expose more open pores? This page puts the project's answers to those three questions for each MOF in one place. Every number on it is read from a result file named under Sources.

0One MOF at a Time

Choose a MOF. The shape is drawn with the same code as the other pages. Cubic MOFs get a Wulff shape from their surface energies; other lattices get the BFDH shape, because the energy step here works for cubic crystals only. Drag to turn it; click a face for its share of the surface and how open it is to gas. A star marks a MOF scored with a close stand-in structure.

Steering the Shape

An additive that binds one face family lowers that family's surface energy, and a face with lower energy grows slower and takes more of the surface. The table gives, for each face, how far its energy must fall before it becomes the main face and before the crystal becomes that face's pure shape. Below the table are the additive types in the laboratory records that gave each face for this MOF, with the papers. Small numbers mean a mild additive should be enough; large numbers mean strong, face-selective binding is needed.

What Each Shape Exposes to a Gas

Each face is cut where the crystal most likely stops (its lowest-energy termination). Every point of that plane is then tested: can a CO2 molecule (radius 1.65 Å) or an N2 molecule (1.82 Å) sit there without touching a framework atom? The open fraction is the share of the face a molecule can enter through. For a whole crystal, the open surface is the average open fraction of its faces, weighted by their areas. The diffusion length is the crystal's volume divided by its surface area, L = V/A; for a sphere it is one third of the radius. All shapes are compared at the same volume, so a rounder shape, with less surface, has a longer L. A shorter L means the crystal fills faster, because the fill time grows with L². More open surface per unit volume means a faster start of uptake. These are geometric indicators of access only: no adsorption isotherm or diffusion coefficient is calculated here.

0How Good Is the Prediction?

The predicted main face was compared with the main face reported in each laboratory record (Against experiment), for every MOF that has a structure file. Two models that use only the structure are scored against two baselines that use the laboratory data. The first baseline guesses the most common face for that MOF in the other papers. The second guesses the most common face among the other MOFs, which is the situation of a new MOF that nobody has grown yet.

How to read this. Without an additive, the Wulff model gets the main face right exactly as often as the most common laboratory answer for that MOF, and it needs no laboratory data. For a MOF that has never been grown, the laboratory baseline is right far less often. BFDH alone is weaker than the Wulff model here. With an additive, every model is right in fewer than half of the records and their ranges overlap, so none of them predicts what an additive does; BFDH happens to score highest. The ranges in brackets are 95 % intervals: with 32 and 44 records, a difference of a few records means little.

0All 138 Dataset Structures

The structure-only steps (symmetry, allowed faces, BFDH shape, overall form) were also run on all 138 CIF files of the first half of the project. These are unvalidated predictions: no laboratory shape is known for these structures.

Most hMOF files are stored without symmetry (space group P1), so the symmetry-based steps see only a triclinic cell and every hMOF structure comes out compact. This says more about how the hypothetical files were written than about the frameworks.

0Limits

  • The surface energies are unrelaxed: every cut bond costs the same 70 kcal/mol and UFF van der Waals terms add 2–7 % (Surface energy), with no solvent or electrostatics. They rank faces; they are not absolute energies, and the steering percentages are relative indications, not additive binding energies.
  • The IRMOF-8 file keeps only P21/c symmetry because of how its naphthalene linkers are ordered, so its faces are not those of an ideal Fm-3m framework.
  • ZIF-67 is built from ZIF-8 by replacing zinc with cobalt; MIL-53(Fe) and UiO-66-NH2 are scored with stand-in structures and reported separately. SNU-70 and Zn4O(FMA)3 have no structure file here and are not predicted.
  • Mg-MOF-74 has a hexagonal lattice (space group R-3); only the BFDH shape is drawn, so its steering is not computed.
  • Plates, spheres and intergrown crystals come from growth kinetics and are outside every equilibrium shape model used here.
