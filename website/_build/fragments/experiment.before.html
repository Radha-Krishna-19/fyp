0What This Page Does

You know
How a set of face energies or growth rates becomes a shape (Learn, Maths), and the three energy models of Phase 3 and Phase 4.
This page adds
What real crystals of these MOFs look like, with the synthesis conditions that produced them; the machine-learned (CHGNet) surface-energy runs; a check of every method against experiment; the chemistry problems found in the slab calculations; and the same pipeline run on CoRE MOF structures of every crystal system.

A predicted shape means little until it is compared with crystals grown in a laboratory. This page collects those crystals, states the conditions they were grown under, scores each method against them, and records what limits the comparison.

Every prediction on this site is for a bare crystal in vacuum: no solvent, no modulator, no temperature. Real crystals are grown in solvent, often with additives, and their shape can depend on those conditions more than on the framework. A match or mismatch below is therefore evidence about the method under that assumption, not a final verdict on it. Four MOFs are too few to rank methods.

0The Experimental Dataset

Two series in the data show why conditions must be recorded. ZIF-8 grown with sodium formate passes from cubes with truncated edges to rhombic dodecahedra over about four hours (Cravillon et al. 2012), so the time of sampling decides the shape. HKUST-1 changes from octahedron to cuboctahedron to cube as the amount of a monocarboxylic acid modulator rises (Umemura et al. 2011). A single “experimental shape” per MOF is therefore a simplification, and the benchmark below uses only additive-free crystals, because no model here contains an additive.

  • ZIF-8 is the one clean validation: every method gives the rhombic dodecahedron that additive-free crystals show and that Cravillon et al. identify as the equilibrium shape. Since BFDH, which uses geometry alone, already gives it, this case does not distinguish the energy models.
  • MOF-5 separates the methods. The crystals are cubes in 8 of 8 records from two laboratories. The bond-counting energies (graph rule, broken-bond Wulff, UFF Wulff) give a cube and BFDH does not. The UFF cube, however, depends on charging a Zn–O bond the same as a C–C bond (Section 5), and the CHGNet proxy gives a truncated octahedron. So the MOF-5 success rests on the bond-counting assumption, which is the next thing to test.
  • HKUST-1: every method makes {111} the largest face, as in the octahedral crystals, but every method also keeps {100} faces that the crystals do not show.
  • UiO-66 has no reliable benchmark. Additive-free reports describe poorly faceted “clusters of square morphology”; the octahedra usually quoted for UiO-66 are grown with a modulator, and two primary open-access papers now confirm them (Butova et al. 2023: benzoic acid, DMF, 120 °C, TEM; Morelli Venturi et al. 2020: acetic acid in green solvents, 120 °C, SEM). Since no model here contains a modulator, the UiO-66 scores should not be counted for or against any method.
  • The hybrid (BFDH and UFF Wulff blended with weight w) has one free parameter. With four MOFs, choosing the w that scores best would fit the test set, so it is shown at w = 0.5 only and is not ranked.

0Machine-Learned Surface Energies (CHGNet)

What the notebook does

The team's MorphoGraph-MOF notebook uses CHGNet, a machine-learned interatomic potential trained on DFT data, to relax the bulk framework and then a set of slabs: layers about 15 Å thick cut parallel to each low-index face, with 20 Å of vacuum. pymatgen generates every distinct termination of each face. It was run on Colab (T4 GPU) for MOF-5, HKUST-1 and UiO-66 with Miller indices up to 1; ZIF-8 was left out because its CIF has no symmetry when the hydrogens are kept.

Why it produced no shape

A slab has two faces, a top one and a bottom one, and its energy gives only their sum:

p = (Eslab − N Ebulk) / A = γtop + γbottom(1)

where

Eslab
energy of the relaxed slab of N atoms
Ebulk
energy per atom of the relaxed bulk
A
area of one face of the slab
γtop, γbottom
surface energies of the two terminations the slab exposes.

The notebook collects one equation (1) per slab and solves for every termination's own γ. In these MOFs every slab had a different termination on each side, and each slab appeared twice (once flipped), so the system has about half as many independent equations as unknowns. The notebook correctly refused to guess, and made no Wulff shape.

A usable number: the cleavage energy

Cutting a crystal makes two faces at once, so (1) is exactly the energy of a cut. Taking the cheapest cut for each plane and giving each face half of it defines the cleavage energy, a standard quantity in surface science:

γcl(hkl) = minterminations p / 2(2)

Because p/2 = (γtop + γbottom)/2 ≥ min(γtop, γbottom), γcl is an upper bound on the lowest termination energy of that plane, never an underestimate. When the crystal has a centre of inversion (Fm-3m: MOF-5, HKUST-1) the two faces of a cut belong to one family, and (2) is the usual cleavage surface energy. When it does not (F-43m UiO-66), a cut gives γ(+) + γ(−) for two different families, which cannot be separated; both get p/2, marked * below. This post-processing is in code/morphology/mofmorph/chgnet_post.py.

0Chemistry Checks on the Slabs

Most slabs cut through linkers

A real MOF surface ends at a metal–ligand bond or at a capped cluster; it does not end halfway through a benzene ring. pymatgen's slab cutter knows nothing about molecules, however, and places the cut by atom position alone. Every relaxed slab was split into connected pieces (bonds from covalent radii × 1.15, long enough to keep Zn–O, Cu–O and Zr–O clusters whole) and each piece smaller than 5% of the slab was named by its formula (slab_check.py).

The loose pieces are hydrogen atoms, oxygen atoms and CH or C2H2 fragments of linkers: the cutter sliced through C–H and C–C bonds in most slabs. During relaxation CHGNet then released whole CO2 molecules from some broken carboxylates. Neither is a surface a crystal would have. An earlier note guessed that the bond cut-off was splitting metal clusters; the formulas show that it almost never does, the exception being two CuO4 pieces in HKUST-1 (a copper atom cut away with its four carboxylate oxygens). The lowest-energy slabs of MOF-5 and UiO-66 are intact, and HKUST-1's {111} value moves from 0.151 to 0.152 J/m² when only intact slabs are used, so the results above survive this check.

The slabs are thin

The bulk-equivalent thickness (the slab's atoms packed at bulk density) is 15–30 Å, one or two layers of building blocks for cells 18–26 Å across, and it differs between faces because pymatgen rounds each slab up to whole layers. Surface energies are trusted only once they stop changing with thickness; that test has not been run, so the CHGNet numbers are not converged.

Two things follow. pymatgen's symmetrize option returns exactly the slabs of the plain cut for these frameworks, so it adds nothing here. Keeping linkers whole leaves a few terminations on the main faces and none on the higher-index ones, and none of those few is symmetric.

The symmetric slabs that do exist

A few slabs in the runs were symmetric as cut. Each gives its termination's own energy exactly, γ = p/2, with no equation to solve. The notebook did not use them, because it merges the two faces only when the slab is still symmetric after relaxation, and the unconstrained relaxation broke the symmetry of every one. Every symmetric termination costs 1.4 to 9 times the cleavage bound of its face. The terminations a crystal would actually show are the cheap ones, and in these frameworks the cheap cuts are asymmetric: a plane that breaks few metal–ligand bonds leaves different groups on its two sides. Symmetric slabs therefore give exact numbers for the wrong terminations and cannot by themselves recover the low-energy faces that decide the shape. For the Wulff shape the cleavage bound remains the more useful quantity. This was found after the symmetric-slab version of the notebook was written, and it lowers what that notebook can be expected to deliver.

0Is the Flat Bond Cost Safe?

The Phase 4 force field charges every cut bond 70 kcal/mol. The bonds a MOF surface actually breaks are mostly metal–ligand coordination bonds, which are weaker than the C–C and C–H bonds inside a linker, so a flat cost may favour the wrong planes. run_bond_sensitivity.py keeps organic bonds at 70 kcal/mol and multiplies every bond to a metal by f.

HKUST-1truncated octahedrontruncated octahedrontruncated octahedron{111} → {111}
UiO-66octahedronoctahedronoctahedron{111}+ → {111}+
ZIF-8rhombic dodecahedrontruncated rhombic dodecahedrontruncated rhombic dodecahedron{110} → {110}

UiO-66 (octahedron) and HKUST-1 (truncated octahedron) do not change. ZIF-8 keeps {110} as its largest face and gains small {100} faces. MOF-5 moves from a cube to a truncated cube and, at f = 0.25, to a truncated octahedron close to a cuboctahedron, with {100} still the largest face. The MOF-5 cube is therefore a consequence of the flat bond cost, and a bond-type-specific cost (or a DFT check of a few MOF-5 faces) is needed before it is quoted as a prediction.

0Limits and Next Steps

  • A DFT check of two or three faces of MOF-5 and UiO-66, where CHGNet and UFF disagree, is the decisive test. The relaxed slab files are in the CHGNet result zips; the intact lowest-energy slabs listed above are the ones to use.
  • A revised CHGNet notebook (notebooks/morphograph_mof/MorphoGraph_MOF_colab_symmetric_slabs.ipynb) keeps linkers whole where possible, treats a slab symmetric as cut as one termination, keeps any termination energy the equations do fix, and fills the rest with the cleavage bound, labelled as an upper bound. It has not yet been run. The dry run and the symmetric-slab table above say what to expect: cleaner slabs, but a shape still set mostly by the cleavage bound.
  • A better route to individual γ is to make the cheap asymmetric slab symmetric by hand: cut at the cheap plane on both sides and cap the exposed metal sites with a solvent or formate molecule, as real surfaces are capped. This needs chemical potentials for the capping group and is a separate piece of work.
  • Thickness convergence. One face of one MOF at 15, 30 and 45 Å would show whether the CHGNet energies are converged.
  • Solvent and modulator. No model here contains them. The additive records in the dataset are the test set for a model that does.
  • ZIF-8 in CHGNet needs a CIF whose symmetry survives with hydrogens, or a notebook option to find symmetry without them.
  • UiO-66 benchmark needs a primary, open-access source showing well-faceted additive-free crystals; the modulated octahedra are now confirmed from two primary papers.
  • Eleven records whose source could not be identified (no DOI, no copy, not found by search) are held in experimental_morphology_unverified.csv and kept out of every table above until their source is confirmed.
