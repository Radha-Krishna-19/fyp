3Part A: The Spectrum Work (before the panel)

The first half of the project tried to describe each MOF by a number computed from its connectivity (the multifractal spectrum width Δα) and to sort MOFs into classes with it. Nine mistakes were found. Their net effect is that the classes and the two published bands were withdrawn, and the method itself (the periodic quotient graph and the spectrum) was kept and corrected.

The straight-line fit that flattened every spectrum

In simple wordsWe drew a best-fit line through the data in the usual way. The paper we were copying draws it differently, and our way hid the very feature we were looking for.
Why we chose itA free-intercept least-squares line is the default in every library, and it fits the points well.
Why it was wrongThe source paper defines τ(q) as the slope of a line forced through the origin. A free intercept absorbs part of the signal, so the apex of the spectrum was flattened and no spectrum passed the validity test (0 of 77).
The technical reasonThe correct estimator is τ = Σxy / Σx² (through the origin). With it 77 of 77 spectra were valid. A good R² on the wrong model says nothing about whether the model is the right one.
How we fixed itRefit through the origin; keep a separate free-intercept R² only as a test of whether a power law exists at all.
Where to see itcode/spectrum_pipeline/code_12_converged_band.py, defect D in its header

Analysing only the "important" nodes

In simple wordsWe picked the most connected building blocks and studied only those. But in a MOF the important blocks never touch each other directly, so there was nothing to study.
Why we chose itIt is natural to take the influential nodes and look at the graph they form.
Why it was wrongMetal clusters are joined to each other only through linkers. The subgraph induced on the influential nodes therefore has no edges, and every distance in it is infinite.
The technical reasonCover the whole graph with boxes and read the probability measure only at the influential nodes (the paper's procedure).
How we fixed itRewrote the box covering to run on the full graph.
Where to see itdocs/project_notes.md defect 2; code_12_converged_band.py

Averaging in the wrong order

In simple wordsWe averaged first and took the logarithm afterwards. Doing it the other way round gives a different number, and ours was far too small.
Why we chose itAveraging before transforming looks harmless and is cheaper.
Why it was wrongln of an average is not the average of ln. Averaging pi(r) over trials before taking logs biased the spectrum narrow by more than a factor of 10.
The technical reasonBuild Z(q, r) per trial, take ln Z per trial, then average the logarithms (Jensen's inequality gives the direction of the bias).
How we fixed itChanged the order and kept a test that fails if it is reversed.
Where to see itdocs/project_notes.md defect 3

Forgetting that a crystal repeats

In simple wordsWe built the graph of one unit cell only. A crystal is that cell repeated in all directions, so bonds that leave the cell and re-enter on the other side were lost.
Why we chose itOne cell is small and easy to handle.
Why it was wrongWithout the periodic images, UiO-66's 12-connected zirconium cluster appeared with the wrong number of neighbours, or the whole cell collapsed to a star.
The technical reasonUse the labelled quotient graph: each edge carries the integer lattice translation t it crosses, so the infinite graph is recovered exactly by tiling.
How we fixed itAdded the translation label to every edge; both the Python and the browser versions use it.
Where to see itcode_09_network_analysis.py; the Network page

Three classes narrower than the measurement error

In simple wordsWe split the structures into "Narrow", "Medium" and "Wide" by cutting the list of numbers into three parts. Two identical structures ended up in different classes, because the cut was finer than the noise in the number.
Why we chose itCutting at the 25th and 75th percentiles is a quick, common way to make classes.
Why it was wrongThe Medium class was 0.032 wide while each structure's error bar was 0.013 to 0.098. hMOF-94 and hMOF-96 are the same graph yet fell in different classes, so the labels described the random seed.
The technical reasonA class boundary is meaningful only if the class width exceeds the uncertainty of the quantity being cut.
How we fixed itThe function that assigns classes now refuses to run when the class width is smaller than the error bar.
Where to see itassign_classes() in code_12_converged_band.py; Results page

A result that was really just "bigger graph"

In simple wordsThe spectrum width grew whenever the graph was bigger. We first read this as a property of the chemistry (for example pore size). Bigger graphs simply give bigger numbers, so the claim fell apart.
Why we chose itThe correlation was strong (r about +0.7) and a strong correlation looks like a finding.
Why it was wrongThe width Δα rises almost exactly linearly in ln(diameter) (R² at least 0.998 on all four nets) with a different slope on each net, so size explains most of the variation and was never held fixed. The pore-size claim and the four-structure real-MOF claim were withdrawn.
The technical reasonAlways report the size-controlled (partial) correlation next to the raw one, and compare structures only at a size where the quantity has stopped moving. It never stopped moving here.
How we fixed itBoth published bands are marked withdrawn on the Results page with their reasons; the numbers stay visible.
Where to see itResults page; data/spectrum_descriptors/size_scaling/

Describing one graph and computing on another

In simple wordsThe text said the spectrum was computed on the simplified building-block graph. The published numbers were actually computed on the full atom graph. The words and the numbers did not match.
Why we chose itThe coarse-grained graph was the intended method, and the notebooks ran without error, so the mismatch was not noticed.
Why it was wrongBoth notebooks called the spectrum on the atomic supercell. The coarse graph only supplied counts. A first correction then wrongly stated "diameter 8 to 12", which belongs to a different run (the atomic graphs have diameters 34 to 104).
The technical reasonFour different estimators exist in the project; each is listed with its graph, influential set and radii in docs/project_notes.md so that they are not mixed again.
How we fixed itDocumented which graph each published number used and withdrew both bands.
Where to see itdocs/project_notes.md, "Which graph each published number was computed on"

Graphs that were too small to measure a scaling law

In simple wordsTo keep the computer fast we capped the number of atoms. That made the graphs so small that there was hardly any range over which a "scaling" could be measured.
Why we chose itA cap on atoms keeps memory low.
Why it was wrongThe cap allowed only about 430 building blocks, a diameter of 8 to 12, less than one decade of radii. One structure was fitted over four points.
The technical reasonTile the quotient graph combinatorially instead of building atoms. Verified that coarse-graining first and replicating second gives the identical graph (full isomorphism check on five cases) while being 48 to 675 times cheaper.
How we fixed itCoarse-grain first; a diameter gate (at least 8) refuses spectra on graphs that are too small.
Where to see itcode/spectrum_pipeline/_prove_ordering_equivalence.py

A sample where most structures were the same graph

In simple wordsMost of our 77 test MOFs turned out to be the same net with different organic parts. Because the simplified graph deletes the organic detail, they looked identical.
Why we chose itThe structures came from one database sample and looked varied by name.
Why it was wrong48 of 76 share 256 nodes, 384 edges and diameter 12 (a 4×4×4 pcu net). A band over such a set is narrow for a trivial reason.
The technical reasonSub-band work needs a deliberate spread of nets (pcu, tbo, sod, fcu, rht), not random sampling.
How we fixed itA guard refuses to report a band unless the sample holds more than one net. One net (rht) could not be found with the tools available, and this is stated.
Where to see itrequire_topological_diversity() in code_12; code_16_topological_diversity.py

4Part B: The Morphology Work (after the panel)

After the panel asked for crystal shapes from the unit cell, a second set of mistakes was made, mostly in the last two weeks while the force-field, CHGNet and experiment parts were joined. Most were found by an independent review of the code and by comparing with real crystals.

Code that only knew cubic crystals

In simple wordsOur first notebook gave wrong shapes when we gave it a MOF that is not cubic. It silently assumed every crystal is a cube-like box.
Why we chose itThe four demonstration MOFs are all cubic, so the labels (100), (110), (111) were hard-wired.
Why it was wrongFor other crystal systems the face names and the symmetry-equivalence rule are different. The notebook printed cubic-style names and the wrong shape for the hMOF-0 test.
The technical reasonFaces must be grouped by the crystal's own point-group operations. The package now finds the symmetry with spglib, converts primitive-cell indices to conventional ones, and names families by that rule.
How we fixed itAdded a guard that stops when there is no cell, then wrote the general version (hybrid.py), checked on eight CoRE MOFs of six crystal systems.
Where to see itmofmorph/hybrid.py; Experiment, "Any framework"

Leaving out the hydrogen atoms in the energy

In simple wordsFor ZIF-8 we computed the energy of a face without the hydrogen atoms. The face looked cheaper than it is, by half.
Why we chose itThe symmetry of ZIF-8 is only found when hydrogens are left out, so the helper dropped them, and the energy code reused that atom list.
Why it was wrongThe {111} cleavage energy of ZIF-8 was 0.312 J/m² and is 0.619 J/m² with hydrogens. The shape did not change, but the number was wrong.
The technical reasonSymmetry and energy need different atom lists. atoms_for_energy rebuilds the full cell for the energy calculation.
How we fixed itFound in the independent review of 2026-10-14; fixed and covered by a test.
Where to see itREVIEW_2026-10-14.md; mofmorph/hybrid.py

Saying CHGNet "will never" give a shape

In simple wordsWe told the team the machine-learning notebook could never give a Wulff shape for MOFs. That was too strong. It gives no shape as written, and there are ways to fix it.
Why we chose itThe notebook returned no surface energies and we generalised from that.
Why it was wrongEach slab gives only γtop + γbottom, and a MOF face has more termination unknowns than independent equations (MOF-5 {111}: 37 unknowns, rank 20). That is a limit of this notebook's procedure, not of CHGNet.
The technical reasonCleavage energy γcl = min p/2 is an upper bound on the lowest termination energy and does give shapes. Other fixes (capped slabs, symmetric slabs) are listed as untested options.
How we fixed itReworded the message, implemented the cleavage workaround, and tested whether symmetric slabs help (they are high-energy terminations, so they do not).
Where to see itExperiment, sections 4 and 5

Trusting the force-field cube because it matched experiment

In simple wordsOur force-field model gave a cube for MOF-5, which is what experiments show, so we treated it as validated. But the cube disappears if one setting is changed.
Why we chose itIt matched the experimental shape, and the machine-learning route disagreed, so the simple model looked better.
Why it was wrongThe Phase 4 energy uses one flat cost of 70 kcal/mol for every bond. At half or a quarter of that cost for metal–ligand bonds the MOF-5 shape becomes a truncated cube or a truncated octahedron. A match found with a free setting is weak evidence.
The technical reasonRun a sensitivity test on every free constant before quoting the result. The decisive test is a DFT calculation on a few faces.
How we fixed itAdded the bond-cost sensitivity test, quote the cube only with this caveat, and wrote the DFT plan for Kshitiz.
Where to see itExperiment section 6; code/morphology/DFT_CHECK_PLAN.md

One "correct" shape per MOF

In simple wordsWe compared our shapes with a single experimental shape per MOF. In the laboratory the same MOF gives different shapes depending on what it is grown in and for how long.
Why we chose itA short table of expected shapes is easy to use.
Why it was wrongZIF-8 changes from cubes to rhombic dodecahedra over about four hours; HKUST-1 changes from octahedron to cube as modulator is added. UiO-66 forms clean octahedra only with a modulator, and our first source for it was a citation, not a primary image.
The technical reasonThe dataset must record solvent, additive, temperature and time. Benchmarks use additive-free crystals because no model here contains an additive.
How we fixed itRohith built a dataset of 134 records from 35 papers with every condition; UiO-66 was then confirmed from two primary papers; eleven records with no identifiable source are held out.
Where to see itdata/experimental_morphology/; Experiment section 2

Misreading broken pieces in the CHGNet slabs

In simple wordsSome computed slabs contained loose pieces. We first thought a metal cluster had been torn in two. It was the organic linker that had been cut.
Why we chose itOur bond-distance rule said the pieces were fragments and we assumed the weakest part is the metal cluster.
Why it was wrongThe loose pieces are H, O, CH or C2H2 and CO2 released from cut linkers. Removing the fragmented slabs changes no shape.
The technical reasonCount the connected pieces before and after relaxation and look at what they are, instead of inferring from the bond rule.
How we fixed itCorrected the statement on the site; the slab check is a script that can be rerun.
Where to see itslab_check.py; Experiment section 5

Small wrong statements about shapes

In simple wordsWe said HKUST-1 is "a cube with cut corners" and that MOF-5 would have cut corners. Neither was right.
Why we chose itRemembered descriptions of typical shapes.
Why it was wrongThe force-field Wulff shape of HKUST-1 is a truncated octahedron (about 77% {111}); the MOF-5 Wulff shape is a clean cube.
The technical reasonAlways read the shape from the computed face fractions, not from memory.
How we fixed itCorrected on the site and in the handoff notes.
Where to see itSurface Energy page

A free weight that can be tuned to the test

In simple wordsThe hybrid rule has one dial, the weight w between BFDH and Wulff. With only four MOFs, any "best" setting is simply the one that happens to suit those four.
Why we chose itIt is tempting to report the weight that scores best.
Why it was wrongWith four test structures and one free parameter, the best w is fitted to the test set, so it says nothing about new MOFs.
The technical reasonChoose w on one set of MOFs and judge it on others (held-out), and show it at a fixed w until then.
How we fixed itThe site shows w = 0.5 only and does not rank it. Held-out testing is the next step.
Where to see itHybrid page

6What Is Still Not Safe

  • The force-field energies are unrelaxed, with no solvent, charges or temperature. They are a baseline, not a measurement.
  • CHGNet has not been checked against DFT for MOF surfaces, so its shapes are not trusted either way until Kshitiz's calculation is done.
  • Only four MOFs have a clean experimental shape; no method can yet be ranked as better.
  • The spectrum width still depends on size (see Spectrum ↔ Shape), and across 55 CoRE MOFs it carries no shape information beyond size; it cannot yet be used as a stand-alone classifier.
