Methodology and Architecture

The full workflow, the system architecture, the datasets, the evaluation strategy, and the justification for each choice.

1Why This Path — and What a Graph Has To Do With a MOF

The commonest question about this project is why a chemistry problem is being attacked with network theory. This section answers that, and the questions that usually follow it.

The chain of reasoning

  1. A MOF is not a molecule — it is a repeating framework. What distinguishes one MOF from another is not only which atoms it contains but how they are wired together.
  2. Wiring is exactly what a graph describes. Collapse each building block to a point and each bond between blocks to a line, and the chemistry reduces to a network of pure connectivity.
  3. The useful properties follow the wiring. Gas moves through the pore network, and the pore network is the empty space left by that wiring. A description of the wiring is therefore a description of what the material can do.
  4. Networks can be measured. Once it is a graph, decades of network science become available — coordination number, centrality, the Laplacian, fractal measures.
  5. A spectrum compresses a whole framework into a few numbers. That is the point: you can compare two MOFs, or ten thousand, without simulating any of them.

The pipeline, stage by stage

Scroll through the six stages — the diagram on the right follows along.

Stage 1

A CIF file, which contains no bonds

Crystallography gives you a unit cell and a list of atom positions — and nothing else. There is no line anywhere saying which atoms are bonded. Every bond in this project was inferred by our own code.

Stage 2

Inferring bonds, periodically

Two atoms are bonded if they are closer than the sum of their covalent radii. But a crystal repeats forever, so the search runs over 27 periodic images and keeps the nearest — otherwise every bond crossing a cell wall is lost.

Stage 3

Cutting the crystal into blocks

The published metal-oxo rule: sever every bond touching a metal, and whatever stays connected is a building block. Blocks with a metal are nodes; the rest are linkers. We use this algorithm unmodified.

Stage 4

Blocks become a graph

Each block collapses to a single vertex, each inter-block bond to an edge. Every edge carries the lattice translation it crosses — drop those labels and UiO-66's twelve connections collapse to one.

Stage 5

Measuring how the network grows

Centre a ball on a block and count what falls inside as the radius grows. Repeat for every influential block, sweep a distortion exponent q, and a Legendre transform turns the result into the spectrum f(α).

Stage 6

One framework, three numbers

Δα is how varied the pore network is, α0 its typical dimension, A its asymmetry. That compression is what lets you compare ten thousand frameworks without simulating any of them.

Why bother, when simulation already exists?

Because simulation does not scale to the size of the problem. A GCMC gas-uptake calculation takes hours per structure, and there are hundreds of thousands of known and hypothetical MOFs. Screening them all directly is not feasible.

A graph descriptor takes seconds. If a cheap structural number can narrow 100,000 candidates to a few hundred worth simulating properly, that is the whole value — not replacing simulation, but deciding what to simulate.

What is the multifractal spectrum actually measuring?

Start with a simpler question: how much network is near a given block? Grow a ball of radius r around it and count what falls inside. Do that for every influential block and you have a set of growth curves.

If a framework were perfectly uniform, every block would grow at the same rate and a single number — a fractal dimension — would describe the whole thing. Real frameworks are not uniform: some regions are tightly tied together, others open into large voids. So instead of one number you need a distribution of growth rates, and that distribution is the spectrum f(α).

QuantityWhat it means in plain terms
αhow fast the network grows around one particular block — a local dimension
f(α)how much of the framework shares that growth rate
α₀ (the apex)the typical growth rate — the framework's dominant dimension
Δα (the width)how varied the framework is. Narrow = uniform throughout. Wide = a mix of tight regions and open ones
A (asymmetry)which side dominates — mostly dense with a few voids, or mostly open with a few dense knots

So a spectrum is a fingerprint of pore-network heterogeneity expressed as a curve, and Δα is that fingerprint reduced to one number — which is what makes a band possible at all.

What we set out to achieve

The goal: establish whether a purely structural, simulation-free descriptor can group MOFs into meaningful families — so a new framework can be placed against a known band and triaged without any expensive calculation.

The honest answer we reached is in Section 17, and it is partly yes and partly no. The spectra are now computed correctly and all 77 frameworks land in a tight, well-defined band. But we can also demonstrate that the width is currently dominated by a computational parameter rather than by chemistry, so the band cannot yet be read as a chemical statement. Establishing exactly what it would take to fix that is the result.

Questions we expect — and the answers

“Isn't this throwing away the chemistry?”
Deliberately, yes. The graph is unweighted — it does not know which element an atom is. That is a real limitation and we say so. It is also the point: whatever survives that stripping is pure architecture, and it transfers across chemistries in a way an element-specific descriptor cannot.

“Does Δα predict gas uptake?”
No, and we never claim it. Nothing here computes adsorption. Uptake comes from GCMC or experiment. Δα describes pore-network architecture only.

“Why only the influential nodes, not all of them?”
Because the paper's iNMFA method restricts the measure to the top-connected 10%, arguing those blocks carry the framework's structure. Section 12 shows this has a catch in a perfect crystal: every node ties, so “top 10%” is ambiguous. We report that rather than hide it.

“Why a periodic graph — why not just use the unit cell?”
Because a crystal has no edges. Drop the lattice translations and every bond crossing a cell face vanishes, the framework fragments, and coordination numbers come out wrong. Section 11 shows UiO-66 reporting 1-connected instead of the published 12.

“What would make this genuinely useful?”
Removing the size confound (Section 16), then testing the descriptor against measured properties on a set with real metal diversity. Both are concrete next steps, not open research questions.

2Everything We Built, and What Each Part Achieved

Four components. For each: what it does, why it exists, what it achieved, and where to see it.

A

The published pipeline, module by module

What: all seven stages of the MOFid pipeline — CIF input, bond assignment, element classification, the three splitting algorithms (metal-oxo, single-node, all-node), centroid simplification, Systre export, identifier assembly — each with the algorithm in plain terms, the real source code, an argument-by-argument reference, live computed numbers, and a synchronised 3D model. 18 steps.
Why: you cannot credibly criticise an algorithm you have not first shown you understand. This establishes the baseline precisely, in the field's own terms.
Achieved: a faithful, runnable Python translation of the published C++, verified against known crystal chemistry on four structures. It doubles as the teaching artefact for the whole project.

B

A visualisation of how the published decomposition works

What: the published metal-oxo algorithm, stepped through module by module, with the real code beside a live 3D model of the structure at that stage.
Why: the decomposition is the step every downstream analysis depends on, and it is normally invisible — a black box that turns a CIF into a list of blocks. Making each stage visible is what lets anyone check that the blocks the network analysis runs on are the right ones.
Achieved: any .cif can be uploaded and the whole pipeline runs on it live in the browser. The node and linker assignments can be inspected directly, which is the sanity check the later sections rest on.

Building-block network analysis

What: treat the decomposed framework as a graph. Build it periodically, measure coordination numbers, compute centrality three ways, take the Laplacian eigenvalue spectrum, and simulate defects.
Why: this is the extension asked for — influential nodes and a spectrum that could characterise a family of materials.
Achieved: correct coordination numbers for all four structures (including UiO-66 at 12, which the naive construction gets wrong); the finding that centrality is degenerate in a perfect crystal; a working defect simulation that makes influence measurable; and Laplacian spectra that cleanly separate the four structures.

4Roadmap

Where the project stands and what the next steps are, including the ones that are blocked and why.

Understand and reproduce the published algorithm done

All seven modules translated to runnable Python and validated against published crystallography on four structures. Python and JavaScript implementations cross-checked and in agreement.

Identify the limitations that remain in it done

Four documented, each with measured evidence rather than assertion: incomplete nodes, oxygen-only bridge chemistry, inferred-bond sensitivity, and silent conventions for rods and solvent.

Propose and implement fixes done

Node completion and donor-agnostic bridging change the decomposition; confidence and convention reporting make the hidden choices visible. Each switchable independently so its effect can be measured on its own.

Build the periodic block graph correctly done

Labelled quotient graph with lattice translations on the edges. Produces literature-correct coordination numbers where the naive construction fails — UiO-66 at 12 rather than 6.

Influential nodes and the spectrum current

Centrality implemented three ways; the degeneracy result established and reported honestly. Laplacian spectra computed and shown to separate the four structures. Defect simulation working — this is the route by which influence becomes a measurable quantity rather than a tie-break.

Scale to a real dataset next — and blocked

This is the gate on the main claim. The goal of the form "all Zr-based MOFs occupy this region of spectral space" cannot be tested with one zirconium structure, however clean the separation looks on four. It needs a few hundred structures grouped by metal, from CoRE MOF or QMOF. Both analysis modules are written per-CIF, so batching them over a folder requires no change to the algorithms — only a loop, and the disk space.

Systematic defect study next

Remove each linker in turn and rank blocks by the damage caused — measured as the drop in λ₂. That produces a genuine influence ranking, grounded in a physical consequence rather than in a tie-break, and connects directly to the well-studied defect chemistry of UiO-66.

Close the loop with topology later

Run the exported .cgd files through Systre and check whether the Laplacian spectrum agrees with the RCSR topology assignment. If two structures share a spectrum, they should share a net — a falsifiable test of whether the fingerprint means what we claim.

What we can and cannot claim today. We can claim: the published algorithm reports chemically incomplete nodes in a specific, measurable way; our fix recovers the correct SBU on three of four test structures and correctly leaves the fourth alone; the periodic graph gives coordination numbers matching published crystallography; centrality is degenerate in defect-free crystals; and the Laplacian spectrum separates our four structures. We cannot yet claim anything about Zr-based MOFs as a family — that requires the dataset above, and saying so is more defensible than overreaching.

5System Architecture

Eight modules, each with one responsibility and a defined interface to the next. Every module is independently runnable and independently testable, which is what let us find the three defects listed further down — a monolithic script would have hidden all of them.

Why two independent implementations

The Python modules and the JavaScript engine were written from the same specification but not from each other. Where they disagree, one of them is wrong — and that disagreement is loud, whereas a single implementation is silently self-consistent even when it is wrong. Two of the three defects we found were caught exactly this way. The cost is roughly a third more code; the benefit is that the numbers on this site are cross-checked rather than asserted.

The honest position on the dataset. The 77 frameworks are computer-generated candidates, not materials anyone has made. They are chemically reasonable — hMOF structures are assembled from real building blocks under real bonding rules, and they are the same class of structure that MOFDiff and comparable models are trained on — but no one has synthesised them. Because all 77 share one metal, the band we report is a band within one chemical family, and cannot yet be claimed to separate families. Every conclusion on this site is written to respect that limit.

Why "confound control" is on this list at all. A structural descriptor that correlates with a physical property is only interesting if it is not simply restating the size of the object. Most descriptors that look promising fail exactly here, and the failure is invisible unless you go looking for it. Putting the test in the evaluation plan before running it is what made the negative result reportable instead of embarrassing.
