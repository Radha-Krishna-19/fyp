1In One Paragraph

A metal–organic framework is a crystal composed of metal clusters connected by organic linkers. Hundreds of thousands of candidate structures have been proposed, and deciding whether one is worth synthesising ordinarily requires a molecular simulation that takes hours per structure. This project asks a narrower, answerable question: does a descriptor computed from a framework's connectivity alone — in seconds, with no simulation — carry enough structural information to be useful for that decision? We construct each framework's periodic graph from its crystal structure, compute the multifractal spectrum of that graph, and reduce each framework to a small set of numbers, principally the spectrum width Δα. Across 138 frameworks — 77 hypothetical and 61 that have been experimentally synthesised — those numbers initially appeared to form a narrow, shared band. A convergence test run after that result showed the band is not stable: Δα keeps growing as the analysed supercell grows, at every range of the exponent q we tested, so the value reported for each framework is a function of an analysis choice rather than a property of the framework itself. Both what held up under testing and what did not are reported below.

The band does not converge, at any tested range of q, and should not be read as a structural result — for two separate reasons. Δα is measured over an exponent q ∈ [−10, 10]; at the extremes of that range the measurement is dominated by a single box, so Δα inherits a term that grows with supercell size and never settles. We tested whether narrowing the q range removes this dependence, on four frameworks spanning four different nets (pcu, tbo, sod, fcu), and it does not: at every cap tried (|q| ≤ 10, 8, 5, 4, 3, 2) Δα continued to drift as the supercell grew, by more than the 5% tolerance we set for calling it settled. Separately, both bands below were in fact computed on the atomic supercell graph rather than the coarse-grained graph this site describes — a graph that spans a molecular and a framework length scale, across which a single power-law fit is not valid regardless of size. The numbers are kept, and the method that produced them documented in full, on the Results page and in the dataset README, exactly as the two withdrawn claims below were kept rather than deleted.
The two sets landed in the same place — which is a weaker fact than it sounds. The band, unconverged and computed on the wrong graph, was calibrated on computer-generated frameworks and applied unchanged to 61 experimentally synthesised MOFs from CoRE MOF 2019. The two sets landed at the same centre (Δα = 0.95–1.47 for the synthesised set against 1.02–1.41 for the hypothetical one; means differ by 0.018, p = 0.38) and the same spectrum shape. That does not show the descriptor transfers between datasets: it shows the same computational artefact was produced twice, on graphs of overlapping size, computed the same way. This replaced an earlier claim, made from four structures, that the two sets did not overlap; that claim was withdrawn on the Results page along with the reason it was wrong.
The pore-size correlation, also withdrawn. Δα correlates with pore diameter at r = −0.497, which looked like a finding. Controlling for the number of blocks in the graph reduces it to r = +0.101, while graph size itself survives at +0.621, and adding pore size to a size-only model improves R² by only 0.005. The descriptor was largely measuring graph size, not pore geometry. We removed that claim rather than presenting it; the reasoning is set out in full in Results.

2The Pipeline, Stage by Stage

Six stages take a crystal structure file to a small set of numbers. Each stage depends only on the ones above it, and each is documented in full on the page it links to.

  1. What a MOF is, and why it matters

    A metal–organic framework is a crystal formed by joining metal clusters (nodes) to organic molecules (linkers), leaving a regular lattice of voids. The internal surface area of a gram of MOF can exceed a football pitch, which is why these materials are candidates for storing hydrogen, capturing CO₂, and separating gas mixtures.

    Establishes: what the material is, and why its porosity is worth engineering.

  2. Cutting a crystal into blocks

    A crystal structure file lists atom positions and a unit cell, and no bonds at all. Reasoning about the framework requires first deciding which atoms are bonded, then grouping them into building blocks: this metal cluster, that linker. This is the published metal-oxo decomposition, used here unmodified, and it is the step that turns a list of atoms into named parts.

    Establishes: what a node is, what a linker is, and how the program finds them without being told.

  3. Turning blocks into a graph — and getting periodicity right

    Collapsing every block to a point and every bond between blocks to a line turns the chemistry into a graph. The complication is that a crystal repeats forever: a bond may leave the unit cell on one side and re-enter on the other. Recording which bonds wrap around gives the correct graph; omitting it does not — UiO-66's metal cluster, which demonstrably connects to twelve linkers, is then reported as connecting to only one.

    Establishes: why the same crystal can produce two different graphs, and which one is correct.

  4. Measuring the graph

    Once the graph is correct, it becomes possible to ask which blocks hold the structure together — centrality. In a perfect crystal the answer is unhelpful: every metal cluster is an exact copy of every other, so none can be ranked above another. Removing one linker breaks that symmetry, and the measurement starts to discriminate. That failure of the naive approach is itself reported as a result.

    Establishes: what centrality measures, and why perfect symmetry defeats it.

  5. The multifractal spectrum

    Covering the graph with boxes of increasing radius and counting what falls in each gives a growth curve per block. A single growth rate would describe a uniform framework; a real framework is not uniform, so a range of growth rates is needed, and plotting how much of the structure follows each one traces an inverted parabola. Its width, Δα, was intended as a single number describing how varied the framework's local environments are — see the caveat above on whether that number is yet trustworthy.

    Establishes: what the parabola on every chart on this site is, and what its width was intended to mean.

  6. The band, the confounds, and what remains open

    Computing this width for 138 frameworks produces values that cluster into a narrow band. Most of the project's effort went into checking whether that band means anything: one apparent finding did not survive the check, one of our own headline claims did not survive a later check, and a third check — whether the band converges with supercell size at all — did not survive either.

    Establishes: what the project established, what it withdrew, and what remains unresolved.

What all six stages are in service of. Deciding whether one MOF is worth making takes hours of molecular simulation, and there are hundreds of thousands of candidates, so simulating all of them is not feasible. The aim here is not to replace that simulation. It is to compute something in seconds, from the crystal structure alone, that is informative enough to decide which few hundred are worth the hours. Every stage above exists to make that number trustworthy — and the most recent finding is that, as currently defined, it is not yet.

3The Problem: What Is a MOF, and Why Does It Matter?

A metal–organic framework is assembled from a small number of component types, in the way a building is assembled from components: an inorganic node (a metal cluster) and an organic linker, which combine into a periodic framework. The properties that make a MOF useful — surface area, pore size, thermal and chemical stability, catalytic behaviour — follow from which components were used and how they are connected.

When a MOF exists only as a crystal structure file, the first question is therefore: what building blocks is it made of? A decomposition algorithm answers that question, and every later use of the result — database search, similarity matching, machine-learning featurisation, inverse design — inherits whatever answer it gives. If the reported node is not the species actually present in the crystal, everything built on that answer is reasoning about a material that does not exist.

A complication that shapes the whole project. A .cif file records atomic positions and the unit cell, and contains no bonds at all; the format has no field for them. Every bond used at any step of this pipeline is therefore inferred from interatomic distances rather than read from the file. That one fact is the origin of several of the limitations described later.

The brief was to find and highlight the influential nodes: those with the highest degree. We implemented this, and the result is more informative than a plain yes or no.

1a · Ranking blocks by degree does not work, and the reason is structural

In a perfect crystal, every symmetry-equivalent block is genuinely identical: every copper paddlewheel in HKUST-1 has exactly the same environment as every other. A "top-k by degree" list therefore discovers nothing; it sorts tied values, and which ones appear first depends only on the arbitrary order in which the blocks were numbered.

See the "distinct degree values" column. Every structure has exactly two distinct values: one for nodes, one for linkers. There is no third tier and no gradient to rank within. A "top 12 most influential" list drawn from these numbers reports sort order, not chemistry.

1b · The degree itself, however, is the most useful number in the analysis

The ranking does not work; the underlying measurement does, and it is not a lesser finding. A block's degree in the periodic graph is its coordination number: the number of connections that building unit makes to the rest of the framework. In reticular chemistry, this number determines which net a given pair of building blocks can form at all — a 4-connected node with a 3-connected linker gives the net tbo; a 6-connected node with a 2-connected linker gives pcu. It is the descriptor around which the field is organised.

The deliverable is the number, not the ranking. Computing these coordination numbers correctly does three things at once: it validates the decomposition, since every value matches published crystallography; it identifies the net, because the node/linker connectivity pair determines the topology; and it separates MOF families, which the spectrum analysis later builds on. What it cannot do is rank identical objects against one another, and establishing that this is not possible is itself a reportable result.
Getting this right was not automatic. The "naive graph" column above shows what a straightforward implementation produces. Because a MOF is infinite, two blocks joined by several bonds running in different lattice directions collapse into a single edge unless the lattice translation is recorded on that edge. UiO-66 exposes this most clearly: its primitive cell holds one Zr₆ cluster whose twelve connections all run out to periodic images and back, so a naive construction reports 6 connections instead of 12, or degenerates into a star with one hub joined to everything. We instead build the labelled quotient graph, which is also exactly what Systre consumes.

1c · Where influence does become a measurable quantity

Symmetry is what flattens centrality; breaking it makes centrality informative. We therefore simulate a missing-linker defect, which is not an artificial test but a real and well-studied feature of MOF chemistry, particularly in UiO-66.

Removing one linker splits the blocks into several genuinely different classes, and λ₂ drops — a measure of how much more easily the framework can now be cut apart, i.e. how much the defect weakened it. This is the point at which "influential nodes" becomes a real research question: which block, if removed, costs the framework the most.
