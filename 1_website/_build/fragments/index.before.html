0In One Paragraph

A metal–organic framework is a crystal built from metal clusters joined by organic struts. Hundreds of thousands of candidate structures exist; deciding whether one is worth making requires a molecular simulation that takes hours per structure. We ask a narrower, answerable question: can a descriptor computed from the framework's connectivity alone — in seconds, with no simulation — carry enough structural information to group frameworks usefully? We build the framework's periodic graph from its crystal structure, compute the multifractal spectrum of that graph, and reduce each framework to three numbers. Across 138 frameworks — 77 hypothetical and 61 that have actually been synthesised — those numbers form a narrow band, and the two sets land on the same band. We then test that band honestly, and report where it fails.

The descriptor transfers to real materials. The band was calibrated entirely on computer-generated frameworks. Sixty-one experimentally synthesised MOFs from CoRE MOF 2019, run through the identical pipeline, land at Δα = 0.95–1.47 against the hypothetical set's 1.02–1.41 — the same centre (means differ by 0.018, p = 0.38) and the same spectrum shape. This replaced an earlier claim, made from four structures, that the two sets did not overlap; that claim is withdrawn on the Results page along with the reason it was wrong.
The result we withdrew. Δα correlates with pore diameter at r = −0.497, which looked like a finding. Controlling for the number of blocks in the graph collapses it to r = +0.101, while graph size itself survives at +0.621, and adding pore size to a size-only model improves R² by 0.005. The descriptor was largely measuring how big the graph was. We removed that claim from the project rather than presenting it. The reasoning is set out in full in Results.

0How to Read This Site, If You Know Nothing About Any of It

You learn a language by letters, then words, then grammar, then sentences, then whole letters home. This project is built the same way, and the site is ordered to match. Each rung below assumes only the rungs above it. Nothing later is comprehensible without the earlier ones, and nothing earlier needs the later ones.

  1. Letters

    What a MOF is, and why anyone cares

    A metal–organic framework is a crystal built by joining metal clusters (nodes) to organic molecules (linkers), leaving a regular lattice of holes. The holes are the point: the internal surface area of a gram of MOF can exceed a football pitch, which is why they are candidates for storing hydrogen, capturing CO₂, and separating gas mixtures.

    After this you can say: what the material is, and why a hole is worth engineering.

  2. Words

    Cutting a crystal into blocks

    A crystal file lists atoms and a box, and no bonds at all. To reason about the framework you must first decide which atoms are bonded, then group them into whole building blocks — this metal cluster, that linker. This is the published metal-oxo decomposition, and it is the step that turns a bag of atoms into nameable parts.

    After this you can say: what a node is, what a linker is, and how a program finds them without being told.

  3. Grammar

    Turning blocks into a graph — and getting periodicity right

    Collapse every block to a single point and every bond between blocks to a line, and the chemistry becomes a graph. The subtlety is that a crystal repeats forever: a bond may leave the box on one side and re-enter on the other. Record which bonds wrap around and you get the right answer; ignore it and UiO-66, a framework whose metal cluster demonstrably touches twelve linkers, comes out touching one.

    After this you can say: why the same crystal can give two completely different graphs, and which one is correct.

  4. Sentences

    Measuring the graph

    With a correct graph you can ask which blocks hold the structure together — centrality. In a perfect crystal the answer is embarrassing: every metal cluster is an exact copy of every other, so nothing can be ranked above anything else. Break one linker and the symmetry dies, and the measurement starts working. That failure is a real result and it is reported as one.

    After this you can say: what centrality measures, and why perfect symmetry defeats it.

  5. Paragraphs

    The multifractal spectrum — the parabola

    Cover the graph with boxes of growing radius and count what falls in each. If the framework were uniform, one number would describe the growth. It is not uniform, so a range of numbers is needed, and plotting how much of the structure follows each growth rate traces an inverted parabola. Its width, Δα, is a single number saying how varied the framework's local environments are.

    After this you can say: what the parabola on every chart here is, and what its width means physically.

  6. Letters home

    The band, the confound, and the goal

    Compute that width for 138 frameworks and the values cluster into a narrow band. Then comes the hard part, which is most of the work: checking that the band is not an accident of something trivial. One apparent finding died this way, and so did one of our own headline claims.

    After this you can say: what the project established, what it withdrew, and what remains unproven.

If you read nothing else, read the goal. Deciding whether one MOF is worth making takes hours of molecular simulation. There are hundreds of thousands of candidates, so simulating them all is arithmetic nobody can afford. The aim here is not to replace that simulation. It is to compute something in seconds, from the crystal structure alone, that is informative enough to decide which few hundred are worth the hours. Every step above exists to make that one number trustworthy.
