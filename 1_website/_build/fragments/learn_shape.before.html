0Three Meanings of “Shape”, and What a MOF Is

You know
That a MOF is a porous crystal (see the Overview).
This section
Separates the three length scales at which people talk about the shape of a MOF, and introduces the four test structures.
Which lets us
State the panel's question precisely: it is about the outer shape of the whole crystal.

A metal–organic framework is built from nodes (small clusters of metal and oxygen atoms) joined by linkers (rigid organic molecules). The pattern repeats in three dimensions and leaves large empty pores. The word “shape” is used at three very different scales:

One crystal contains billions of identical unit cells. The same unit cell can be stacked into an octahedron, a cube, a plate or a rod. Which shape appears depends on how fast each flat face of the crystal grows.

0Lattices and Unit Cells

You know
That a crystal repeats.
This section
Defines the lattice, the unit cell, fractional coordinates, and the difference between a primitive and a conventional cell.
Which lets us
Read a CIF file and convert it to the cell in which crystal faces are named.

A lattice is the set of all points r = ua + vb + wc with whole numbers u, v, w. The three vectors a, b, c are the lattice vectors, and the box they span is a unit cell. The atoms inside one cell are the basis; copying the basis to every lattice point rebuilds the whole crystal. A cell is described by six numbers, the lengths a, b, c and the angles α, β, γ. These are the “unit cell dimensions” in the panel's question.

Positions inside the cell are written as fractional coordinates (x, y, z), meaning r = xa + yb + zc with 0 ≤ x, y, z < 1. CIF files store atoms this way.

The HKUST-1 and UiO-66 CIFs store the primitive cell: 18.59 Å and 14.51 Å with 60° angles. They look non-cubic, but both crystals are cubic. Their conventional cubic cells are 26.30 Å and 20.52 Å (aconv = √2 aprim for an F-centred lattice). Faces must always be named in the conventional cell.

Put the lattice vectors as the rows of a 3×3 matrix L. The metric tensor collects all their dot products:

G = L LT,    |r|2 = uTG u(1)

where

L
matrix whose rows are a, b, c
u
the fractional coordinates of a lattice vector r
G
the metric tensor; for a cubic cell G = a²I

0Crystal Planes, Miller Indices and Spacing

You know
The lattice and the unit cell.
This section
Names families of parallel planes, and computes how far apart they are.
Which lets us
Name the faces of a crystal, because every flat face is parallel to one family of lattice planes.

A plane that cuts the three axes at a/h, b/k and c/l has Miller indices (hkl), reduced to have no common factor. A zero means the plane is parallel to that axis; a negative index is written with a bar, (1̅10). Curly brackets name a whole symmetry family: {100} means all six cube faces.

A cube is bounded by the six {100} planes, an octahedron by the eight {111} planes, and a rhombic dodecahedron by the twelve {110} planes. Naming the faces that survive is naming the shape.

A family (hkl) is a stack of equally spaced planes. Their spacing dhkl shrinks as the indices grow:

The general formula uses the reciprocal lattice. Let B = (L−1)T; its rows a*, b*, c* satisfy a·a* = 1, a·b* = 0 and so on. The vector ghkl = ha* + kb* + lc* is perpendicular to the planes, and

1 / dhkl2 = |ghkl|2 = hTG−1h,    cubic: dhkl = a / √(h2 + k2 + l2)(2)
Worked example. MOF-5 has a = 25.866 Å. Then d111 = 25.866/√3 = 14.93 Å, d200 = 25.866/2 = 12.93 Å and d220 = 25.866/√8 = 9.15 Å.

0Symmetry, and Why Some Faces Cannot Exist

You know
How to name planes and compute their spacing.
This section
Explains symmetry operations and space groups, and proves the rule that forbids some plane families.
Which lets us
Compute the true spacing of each face, which sets how fast it grows in the simplest model.

A symmetry operation maps the crystal onto itself. In fractional coordinates it always has the form x → Rx + t, where R is a rotation, reflection or inversion (an integer matrix) and t a translation. Pure fractional translations are called centring: F-centring adds (0, ½, ½), (½, 0, ½), (½, ½, 0); I-centring adds (½, ½, ½). The full set of operations is the crystal's space group; there are 230, with names such as Fm-3m (number 225), whose first letter gives the centring.

Centring places identical planes halfway between the planes of a family. Waves scattered from the two sets cancel exactly, so the family is systematically absent and the true repeat distance is half the naive one. The general rule is proved on the Mathematics page:

(hkl) is absent if some operation has hR = h and h·t is not a whole number(3)

For centring alone this gives: F-centred, h, k, l all even or all odd; I-centred, h + k + l even. If (hkl) is absent and n(hkl) is its first allowed multiple, the effective spacing is deff = dhkl/n.

All four CIFs are stored in space group P1, with no symmetry written. The project finds the true group with the spglib library: MOF-5 and HKUST-1 Fm-3m, UiO-66 F-43m, ZIF-8 I-43m. For ZIF-8 the hydrogen positions in the file break the symmetry, so it is found only with the hydrogens removed.

0How a Crystal Gets Its Shape: the Slowest Faces Win

You know
Which faces a crystal can have.
This section
Shows why a growing crystal ends up bounded by its slowest-growing faces, and turns that into one formula for the shape.
Which lets us
Predict a shape from any estimate of the face growth rates.

Each face moves outward at its own normal growth rate Rf. In the simulation below a crystal starts with four slow faces and four fast ones:

After long growth, the distance of each face from the centre is proportional to its rate, so the crystal is the region

P = { x : nf·x ≤ Rf for every face f }(4)

where

nf
unit vector perpendicular to face f (the direction of ghkl)
Rf
growth rate of face f, used as its distance from the centre
P
the crystal shape: an intersection of half-spaces, always a convex polyhedron (a polytope)
Every shape model in this project uses formula (4). They differ only in how the numbers Rf are estimated: from the lattice (BFDH), from surface energies (Wulff), or from the periodic graph (Phase 3).

0BFDH: Shape from the Unit Cell Alone

You know
That shape follows from the growth rates.
This section
Gives the oldest rule for the rates, which uses only the lattice and the space group.
Which lets us
Answer the panel's question with the exact input they specified.

The rule named after Bravais, Friedel, Donnay and Harker says that widely spaced planes are densely packed and strongly bonded within themselves, so they grow slowly:

Rhkl ∝ 1 / deff(hkl)(5)

ZIF-8 crystals are indeed rhombic dodecahedra in the laboratory, and HKUST-1 and UiO-66 are usually octahedral. MOF-5, however, is usually reported as cubes, which BFDH does not predict. That gap is where the framework's connectivity matters, as the Graph Faces page shows.

0Wulff Shape and Attachment Energy: Shape from Energy

You know
The lattice-only rule and its limits.
This section
Introduces two energy-based rules: the equilibrium (Wulff) shape and the attachment-energy growth shape.
Which lets us
Bring chemistry, or its graph version, into the growth rates.

The surface energy γhkl is the energy cost per unit area of creating face (hkl). A crystal at equilibrium minimises its total surface energy for a fixed volume. Wulff's theorem says the winning shape is formula (4) with Rf replaced by γf:

W = { x : n·x ≤ γ(n) for every direction n }(6)

For growth rather than equilibrium, cut the crystal into slices of thickness dhkl. The energy released when a new slice attaches is the attachment energy Eatt; Hartman and Bennema (1980) proposed that the growth rate is proportional to it, Rhkl ∝ Eatt. A face that gains little when a slice attaches grows slowly. On a graph, energy can be counted as links, which is exactly what Phase 3 does.

0Bond Chains: Flat, Stepped and Kinked Faces

You know
That slow faces survive, and that bonds set the speed.
This section
Explains Hartman–Perdok theory, which sorts faces into three classes by the chains of strong bonds inside one slice.
Which lets us
Predict which faces can appear at all, from the bond network alone.

A periodic bond chain (PBC) is an unbroken chain of strong bonds that repeats with a lattice translation. Take the slice of thickness dhkl parallel to a face and look at the chains inside it:

  • F face (flat): chains in two or more directions, so the slice is a connected sheet. Growth needs a whole new layer to start, so it is slow. F faces dominate crystal shapes.
  • S face (stepped): chains in one direction only. Growth is faster.
  • K face (kinked): no chain inside the slice. Every site is a kink, so the face grows fastest and usually disappears.

0Periodic Graphs, and How They See Crystal Faces

You know
Faces, bond chains, and (from the Network page) graphs.
This section
Connects the labelled quotient graph built earlier in the project to crystal faces.
Which lets us
Compute face classes and growth rates from the framework's connectivity, which is Phase 3.

The labelled quotient graph has one vertex per building block in one unit cell. An edge (i, j, t) says block i in this cell is linked to block j in the cell shifted by the lattice vector t, called the edge's voltage. Unfolding the graph with its voltages rebuilds the infinite framework.

A periodic bond chain is a cycle in the quotient graph whose voltages add up to a non-zero lattice vector τ. It lies in face (hkl) when hτ1 + kτ2 + lτ3 = 0. Counting the independent such directions inside one slice (its periodicity rank) gives the face class: 2 is F, 1 is S, 0 is K. The Graph Faces page computes this for all four MOFs.

0Node Shapes: Geometric Signatures

You know
That shape exists at the node scale too.
This section
Introduces the shape vocabulary for building units used by Wu and Jiang (Chem. Sci. 2024).
Which lets us
Describe tetrahedral, square or octahedral nodes precisely when we discuss chemistry.

Wu and Jiang classify every building unit by the arrangement of its connection points into one of 13 prototypes, using the number of connection points, a planarity test and the smallest root-mean-square deviation from ideal shapes. The Cu paddlewheel of HKUST-1 is a square (S4) and BTC a triangle (T3); together they form the tbo net.
