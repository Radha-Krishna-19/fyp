0What Is Proved Here

The ideas on the Learn: Crystal Shape page.
This page
States each rule used by the morphology work as a proposition and proves it. This is Phase 2 of the plan.
Which lets us
Claim that the morphologies on the Morphology page follow mathematically from the unit cell, and are not just pictures.

The chain of reasoning is: symmetry decides which faces exist (Propositions 1–2); growth rates turn faces into a convex shape with the crystal's symmetry (3–5); the shape changes continuously, through exact named thresholds, as the rates change (6–8); the shapes can be told apart by numbers, and one popular number cannot do it (9–10); and the periodic graph gives growth rates and face classes (11–13). Results that are classical are cited rather than re-proved, and say so. The last section checks every proposition numerically in your browser.

h = (h, k, l) is a row vector of Miller indices. x is a column vector of fractional coordinates. A symmetry operation is (R, t), acting as x → Rx + t. nf is the outward unit normal of face f and Rf > 0 its distance from the centre. P(R) = {x : nf·x ≤ Rf for all f}.

0Propositions 1–2: Which Faces Exist

Let F(h) = Σj fj e2πi h·xj be the structure factor of reflection h, summed over the atoms j of the cell. If the space group contains (R, t) with hR = h and h·t ∉ ℤ, then F(h) = 0.

Proof. The operation maps the set of atoms onto itself, so summing over the images Rxj + t gives the same sum: F(h) = Σ fj e2πi h·(Rxj+t) = e2πi h·t Σ fj e2πi (hR)·xj = e2πi h·t F(hR). With hR = h this reads F(h)(1 − e2πi h·t) = 0. The bracket is non-zero because h·t is not an integer, so F(h) = 0. ∎

(a) F-centring forbids (hkl) unless h, k, l are all even or all odd. (b) I-centring forbids (hkl) unless h + k + l is even. (c) Hence for F-centred cubic crystals deff(100) = a/2, deff(110) = a/(2√2), deff(111) = a/√3, and for I-centred deff(100) = a/2, deff(110) = a/√2, deff(111) = a/(2√3).

Proof. Centring operations have R = I, so hR = h always and Proposition 1 applies. (a) The F translations give h·t = (k+l)/2, (h+l)/2, (h+k)/2; all three are integers exactly when h, k, l have the same parity. (b) The I translation gives (h+k+l)/2. (c) Apply (a) and (b) to the first multiples: for F, (100) and (110) fail and (200), (220) pass, while (111) passes; for I, (100) and (111) fail and (200), (222) pass, while (110) passes. Then use dhkl = a/√(h²+k²+l²) for the first allowed multiple. ∎

The project's Python code applies Proposition 1 with every operation of the space group, not only centring, so screw axes and glide planes are also handled. It is tested on diamond silicon (Fd-3m), where a glide plane makes the first allowed reflection along (100) the (400), so deff(100) = a/4.

0Propositions 3–5: The Shape Is a Symmetric Convex Polytope

If every Rf > 0 and the normals nf positively span space (every vector is a combination of them with non-negative coefficients), then P(R) is a bounded convex polytope with the origin in its interior.

Proof. Each set {x : nf·x ≤ Rf} is a half-space, which is convex, and an intersection of convex sets is convex. The origin satisfies every inequality strictly because Rf > 0, so a small ball around it lies inside. Suppose P were unbounded. A convex set containing the origin that is unbounded contains a whole ray {sx : s ≥ 0} with x ≠ 0, which forces nf·x ≤ 0 for every f. Write x = Σ μfnf with μf ≥ 0, which positive spanning allows. Then |x|² = Σ μf (nf·x) ≤ 0, so x = 0, a contradiction. ∎

The cubic face families always satisfy the hypothesis: the six {100} normals alone positively span space, and so do the eight {111} normals and the twelve {110} normals.

Let S be a rotation or reflection of the crystal's point group. If the rates are equal on every symmetry family (Rf = RS(f)), then S(P) = P.

Proof. S is orthogonal, so nf·x = (Snf)·(Sx). If x ∈ P, then for every face g = S(f) we have ng·Sx = nf·x ≤ Rf = Rg. Since S permutes the faces, this covers every face, so Sx ∈ P. Applying the same to S−1 gives equality. ∎

A crystal that starts as any bounded seed containing the origin, whose faces then move at constant speeds Rf, has shape P(r0 + tR) at time t, and P(r0 + tR)/t → P(R) as t → ∞. So the long-time shape is formula (4) of the Learn page, whatever the seed.

Proof. Scaling a polytope multiplies all its distances: P(b)/t = P(b/t). Here b/t = r0/t + R → R, and Proposition 6 below shows the polytope depends continuously on its distances. ∎

0Proposition 6: The Shape Changes Continuously

Fix the normals. Let m = minf Rf > 0 and let R′ satisfy |R′f − Rf| ≤ ε < m for every f. Then (1 − ε/m) P(R) ⊆ P(R′) ⊆ (1 + ε/m) P(R), so the Hausdorff distance between the two shapes is at most (ε/m)·maxx∈P|x|. Volume, surface area and the gyration tensor therefore change continuously too.

Proof. Since every Rf ≥ m, we have ε ≤ (ε/m)Rf, so (1 − ε/m)Rf ≤ R′f ≤ (1 + ε/m)Rf for every face. Enlarging every distance can only enlarge the polytope, and P(λR) = λP(R) for λ > 0, which gives the two inclusions. Any point of the larger set is within (ε/m)|x| of the smaller, which bounds the Hausdorff distance. Volume and area are continuous under Hausdorff convergence of convex bodies, and the gyration integral is continuous in the body. ∎

Changing the growth rates smoothly changes the shape smoothly. There are no jumps: between any two morphologies of one unit cell there is a continuous path of morphologies. That is the precise sense in which one unit cell morphs.

0Propositions 7–8: Exact Thresholds of the Named Shapes

With the six {100} faces at distance 1 and the eight {111} faces at distance t, the shape is a cube for t ≥ √3, a truncated cube for 2/√3 < t < √3, a cuboctahedron at t = 2/√3, a truncated octahedron for 1/√3 < t < 2/√3, and an octahedron for t ≤ 1/√3.

Proof. The {111} plane with normal (1,1,1)/√3 is x+y+z = √3t. (i) The cube [−1,1]³ has its farthest point along (1,1,1) at the corner (1,1,1), where x+y+z = 3. The plane misses the cube exactly when √3t ≥ 3, that is t ≥ √3; by symmetry the same holds for all eight corners. (ii) For √3t < 3 the plane cuts a triangle off each corner. The triangle reaches the midpoint (1,1,0) of a cube edge when √3t = 2. At that value neighbouring triangles meet at every edge midpoint, the original square faces have become squares rotated by 45°, and the shape has 12 vertices, all edge midpoints: a cuboctahedron. (iii) The octahedron |x|+|y|+|z| ≤ √3t has vertex (√3t, 0, 0). The cube face x = 1 misses it exactly when √3t ≤ 1, that is t ≤ 1/√3. (iv) Between these values both families have faces of positive area, and the larger family names the shape. ∎

In point group -43m the {111} faces split into two sets of four, {111}+ at distance r+ and {111}− at distance r−. Ignoring other faces, the shape is a regular tetrahedron exactly when r+ ≤ r−/3.

Proof. The four {111}+ normals point to alternate corners of a cube and sum to zero. The tetrahedron they bound with in-radius r+ has its vertex opposite face nk at −3r+nk, because a regular tetrahedron's circumradius is three times its in-radius. The {111}− planes have normals −nk, and at that vertex (−nk)·(−3r+nk) = 3r+. So no {111}− plane cuts the tetrahedron exactly when 3r+ ≤ r−. ∎

Proposition 8 needs a point group without an inversion centre. Our UiO-66 file (F-43m, ordered μ3-OH groups) has one; the averaged Fm-3m structure usually reported does not, and there the two halves are equivalent and a tetrahedron is impossible by Proposition 4.

0Propositions 9–10: Which Numbers Can Tell Shapes Apart

If a body has the rotational symmetry of a cube or of a regular tetrahedron, its gyration tensor is a multiple of the identity, so κ² = 0.

Proof. Symmetry gives S = Q S QT for every symmetry rotation Q. Both groups contain the 180° rotations about the x, y and z axes. The rotation about z changes the sign of x and y, so it maps Sxz to −Sxz and Syz to −Syz; invariance makes both zero, and the other two rotations remove Sxy. Both groups also contain the 120° rotation about (1,1,1), which permutes x → y → z, so the three diagonal entries are equal. Then λ1 = λ2 = λ3 and formula (8) of the Learn page gives 0. ∎

Define Ql2 = (4π/(2l+1)) Σm |Σi wi Ylm(ni)|2 with area fractions wi. Then (a) Ql2 = Σi,j wiwj Pl(ni·nj), and (b) if the shape has an inversion centre, Ql = 0 for every odd l.

Proof. (a) Expand the square and use the addition theorem for spherical harmonics, Σm Ylm(a)Ylm*(b) = ((2l+1)/4π) Pl(a·b). (b) With an inversion centre, every face n has an opposite face −n of equal area. Since Ylm(−n) = (−1)lYlm(n), the pair contributes wYlm(n)(1 + (−1)l) = 0 for odd l. ∎

Formula (a) is what the browser engine uses: it needs only dot products and Legendre polynomials. The Python code computes the original definition with spherical harmonics; the two agree, which checks both.

0Propositions 11–13: Faces from the Periodic Graph

These are the mathematical basis of Phase 3. A periodic graph is given by its quotient graph: vertices with positions in one cell, and edges (i, j, t) with voltage t.

A closed walk in the quotient graph whose voltages sum to τ ≠ 0 lifts to an infinite chain of links in the crystal that repeats with translation τ. The chain is parallel to face (hkl) if and only if hτ1 + kτ2 + lτ3 = 0.

Proof. Lifting an edge adds its voltage to the cell index, so after one trip round the walk the same vertex is reached in the cell shifted by τ; repeating the walk gives an infinite chain with period τ. A lattice vector lies in the plane (hkl) exactly when it is perpendicular to ghkl, and ghkl·τ = hτ1 + kτ2 + lτ3 because the reciprocal basis satisfies a*·a = 1 and a*·b = 0. ∎

In a net whose vertices form a lattice (every vertex equivalent by translation), take one-layer slices parallel to (hkl). A vertex of a new layer forms Eatt = (deg − c0)/2 links with the crystal below, where deg is the vertex degree and c0 the number of its neighbours in its own layer. For pcu this gives 1, 2, 3 on (100), (110), (111); for fcu, 4, 5, 3.

Proof. In a lattice net the neighbour vectors come in pairs v, −v (if a vertex links to the one at v, translating by −v shows it also links to the one at −v). Neighbours not in the layer therefore split equally between above and below, giving (deg − c0)/2 below. pcu: deg 6, and c0 = 4, 2, 0 on (100), (110), (111). fcu: deg 12, and the neighbour vectors (±½, ±½, 0) and permutations give c0 = 4, 2, 6. ∎

Let D be the spacing of lattice planes perpendicular to n and V the cell volume. Translating the crystal by a lattice vector shifts every height n·x by a multiple of D, and each height level holds D/V lattice translates per unit area. Hence any count per unit area (vertices in a slice, links crossing a plane) equals D/V times a finite sum, over the vertices or edges of one cell, of the number of integers k that put the shifted copy in the required height range.

Proof. The heights n·R of lattice vectors R form the set Dℤ. The lattice vectors at one height form a 2D lattice in that plane whose cell has area V/D, because stacking such layers at spacing D must reproduce one lattice point per volume V. A count per unit area is therefore the count per 2D cell divided by V/D, and the count per 2D cell is the number of (object, k) pairs in range. ∎

Proposition 13 lets the Graph Faces page compute attachment numbers and broken-bond surface energies exactly, with no finite crystal and no rounding from cutting a cluster. Applied to a nearest-neighbour fcc crystal it reproduces the classical ratio γ100/γ111 = 2/√3.

0Propositions 14–16: Changing the Unit Cell

These propositions answer the second reading of the panel's question: change the cell parameters a, b, c, α, β, γ of an established MOF and ask what shapes follow. Keep every atom at its fractional coordinates and replace the lattice L by L′ = L(I + ε)T, where ε is a strain. This is a new, hypothetical framework derived from the real one; the propositions say exactly what carries over from the original and what changes.

The structure factor F(h) = Σj fj e2πi h·xj depends only on the fractional coordinates xj and the atom types. Hence the set of forbidden reflections, and the order n(hkl) of every face, are the same for every deformation of the lattice.

Proof. Deforming the lattice changes the Cartesian positions rj = L′Txj and the reciprocal vectors q = L′−1h, but the phase is q·rj = h·xj, unchanged. The symmetry operations (R, t) written in fractional coordinates still map the atom set to itself, so the forbidden reflections they imply stay forbidden. ∎

Let G′ = L′L′T be the new metric tensor and H = {(R, t) in the original space group : RTG′R = G′}. Two faces have the same spacing and the same energy for every isometry-invariant model if they are related by H. The family of a face is its H-orbit, of size |H|/|stabiliser|. A stretch of one cubic axis reduces the point group from m⁻3m (48 operations) to 4/mmm (16), and splits {100} (6 faces) into {100} (4 faces) and {001} (2 faces).

Proof. An operation with RTG′R = G′ preserves lengths, so it maps lattice planes to planes with equal spacing, and maps the (unchanged, Prop. 14) structure onto itself; this is exactly what makes two faces physically interchangeable. An operation that fails the condition changes some lengths, so it is not a symmetry of the deformed crystal and generally maps a plane to one of different spacing. Orbit sizes follow from the orbit–stabiliser theorem: in 4/mmm the stabiliser of (001) has 8 elements and that of (100) has 4, giving orbits of size 16/8 = 2 and 16/4 = 4. ∎

In an orthogonal cell with edges a1, a2, a3 and axial orders n1, n2, n3, the BFDH distances of the axial faces are Ri = dmaxni/ai. The BFDH polytope is exactly the box with these half-widths unless some used plane (hkl) has order nf < Σi wini, where wi = (hi/ai)2 / Σj(hj/aj)2. For a primitive cell every n = 1, so the shape is a box for every deformation, and stretching c by λ flattens it by exactly 1/λ.

Proof. The unit normal of plane (hkl) is n = d(h/a1, k/a2, l/a3) with d = (Σhi2/ai2)−1/2. The box with half-widths Ri reaches out to n·v = Σ|ni|Ri = d dmax Σnihi2/ai2 in direction n. The plane lies at Rf = dmaxnf/d. It cuts the box exactly when Rf is smaller than that reach, that is when nf < d2Σnihi2/ai2 = Σwini. If no plane cuts, the polytope is the box. ∎

Consequences. (i) For F-centred cubic parents (MOF-5, HKUST-1, UiO-66) the axial order is 2 but {111} has order 1, so the {111} planes cut the box for every stretch: the shape stays a truncated box, which is why the curves in the figure bend away from the 1/λ line when the crystal is made taller. (ii) For the I-centred ZIF-8 the {110} planes (order 1) cut instead, giving the rhombic-dodecahedron family. (iii) A lattice-only rule cannot tell MOF-5, HKUST-1 and UiO-66 apart (same lattice and centring): the three curves coincide. The graph and energy rules of Phases 3 and 4 are what separate them, and they have not yet been recomputed for deformed cells.

Proposition 16 is checked on 75 random cells for P, I and F lattices by 2_python/morphology/tests_deform.py: the shape is a box exactly when the criterion says so (25 boxes, 50 cut shapes). The live page Cell Deform lets you change a, b, c and watch it.

0Classical Results Used Without Proof

  • Wulff's theorem: among bodies of fixed volume, the minimum of ∫γ(n) dA is attained by the Wulff shape. Wulff (1901); rigorous proofs by J. E. Taylor (1970s); review by Miracle-Solé, arXiv:1307.5180.
  • BFDH law (R ∝ 1/deff) and Hartman–Bennema (R ∝ Eatt): empirical growth laws, not theorems. They are inputs to the model, and the comparison with experiment on the Morphology page is how they are tested.
  • Continuity of volume and area under Hausdorff convergence of convex bodies: standard convex geometry.
